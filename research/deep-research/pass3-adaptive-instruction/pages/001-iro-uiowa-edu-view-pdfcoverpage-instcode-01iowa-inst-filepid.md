Description of the Musical Aptitude Profile
The Musical Aptitude Profile is a unique musical aptitude test 
which measures melody, harmony, rhythm, and musical sensitivity in a 
purely musical fashion.  The basic musical factors measured by MAP are 
classified into three total tests:  Tonal Imagery, Rhythm Imagery, and
Musical Sensitivity.  There are two separate subtests in each of the 
nonpreference total tests:  Tonal imagery and Rhythm Imagery.  They are
Melody and Harmony for the former and Tempo and Meter for the latter.
The preference test, Musical Sensitivity, consists of three separate 
subtests.  They are Phrasing, Balance, and Style.  The entire battery 
of seven tests,  including practice selections and directions, is 
recorded on high fidelity magnetic tape.  The tests consist of short 
original musical selectiohs,  composed for violin and cello by the author, 
and are performed by professional musicians.  There are 250 items in the 
entire battery.
MAP was nationally standardized during the I96I4.-65 school year.
A total of 12,805 students enrolled in Grades 1; through 12 in 20 school 
systems in 18 states were included in the norms sample.  Since the test 
battery was administered to all students in every participating school, 
the sample upon which the grade norms were based represents the natural 
proportions of musically select and unselect students.
Normalized standard scores and percentile rank equivalents for each 
of the seven subtests of MAP are based on the test results of all of the 
students who participated in the standardization program.  The standard 
score scale has a mean of 50 and a standard deviation of 10.

Comparison of the Related Study with the Present Study 
All of the general musical behaviors which were evaluated in the 
Lincolnwood-Rochester study were also evaluated in the present study. 
Although both the Lincolnwood-Rochester and the present study employed
J
criteria for evaluation related to the constructs of musical aptitude 
embodied in MAP, the latter study utilized criteria which go beyond the 
scope of the seven criteria of the former study.  The present study 
included criteria which were designed to evaluate musical behaviors 
which are peculiar to instrumental music.  These included the ability 
to sight-read rhythmic patterns and demonstrate rhythmic understanding. 
Further, musical behaviors which are unique to this study were included. 
These behaviors embodied students&#39;  abilities to improvise a variation, 
create a new ending, and improvise a lower part to a given melody.
In the related study, music teachers&#39;  subjective ratings were used

19
for purposes of evaluating students’ abilities.  However, in the present 
study individual tape-recorded performances were evaluated by two im­
partial judges.  This enabled the investigator to obtain a more objective 
evaluation of students’ performance as well as to obtain the reliability 
of evaluations which could be used for interpretive purposes.  In addi­
tion, the validity of the evaluations is increased because two judges 
rather than one judge, as in the related study, were employed.  The use 
of“tape-recorded performances for evaluative purposes also made it 
possible for the judges to listen many times to a performance before 
assigning a rating.
Another significant difference between the two studies is that in 
the present study, a performance criterion consisting of specially com­
posed musical etudes was utilized.  The musical etudes were designed for 
the purpose of eliciting specific responses indicative of the capabilities 
of the students with respect to each musical behavior being investigated. 
In the Lincolnwood-Rochester study, however, musical examples were chosen 
because of the students&#39; familiarity with them rather than for the 
quality of the examples for eliciting specific musical responses.  All 
of the musical examples employed in the present study, with the excep­
tion of the sight-reading example, were made available to the students 
prior to the tape recording of their performances.  This afforded each 
student an opportunity to familiarize himself with the examples and to 
work individually on the performance problems attendant to each example.
Finally, the present study was limited to the investigation of the 
diagnostic properties of MAP with respect to instrumental music students. 
Unlike the related study, the present study was not designed to offer

20
specific suggestions regarding methods and procedures which might be 
employed for adapting instruction to meet the individual needs and 
abilities of students.

CHAPTER III 
METHODS AND PROCEDURES
Description of the Students 
In December, 1966, MAP was administered to 123 students enrolled 
in the University Bands at South Dakota State University at Brookings. 
Seventy-three of these students were members of the more advanced 
Symphonic Band and fifty performed in the Concert Band.  Of these 123 
students, approximately twenty percent were music majors and the re­
mainder were working toward a baccalaureate degree in various disci­
plines in the arts and sciences.  The students constituted a cross- 
section of freshmen, sophomores, juniors, and seniors.
Although there were 1J&gt;5&gt; students who were members of the Symphonic 
and Concert Bands, not all members of these organizations participated 
in the study.  Some were excluded because they did not demonstrate suf­
ficient technical competence on a brass or woodwind instrument.  Others 
were eliminated because they performed on percussion instruments for 
which there were no performance criteria prepared.  The 123 students who 
did participate in the study evidenced considerable variability in 
regard to their various overall musical and non-musical attainments.
The Testing Procedure 
A large choral rehearsal room which could accommodate the entire 
group of subjects was utilized for the administration of MAP.  Although

22
the room was somewhat crowded, testing conditions were otherwise excel­
lent.  The investigator determined that the test could be heard clearly 
in all sections of the room and was satisfied that the room was quiet 
and free from distracting noises.
The investigator administered the entire MAP battery in two testing 
sessions.  The first testing session was preceded by a brief introduction 
explaining the test and the nature of the study.  The Tonal Imagery and 
Rhythm Imagery tests were then administered to all 123 students, with a 
ten-minute rest period allowed between the two total tests.  The final 
test, Musical Sensitivity, was administered to all of the students, 
under the same conditions, two days later.  The test tape recordings 
were played on a high quality Ampex tape recorder.
Development of the Criterion
To determine the musical strengths and weaknesses  of brass  and
woodwind  students, performance criteria had to be established.   The
criteria utilized for this study consisted of seven musical examples, 
composed by the investigator, which were especially designed to measure 
the following eleven musical abilities related to instrumental perform­
ance:
1.   Ability to   improvise a variation
2.   Ability to   create a new ending
3.   Ability to   improvise a lower part to a given melody
U.   Ability to   perform with consistent tempo
5&gt;.  Ability to demonstrate rhythmic understanding
6.  Ability to comprehend meter
7.  Ability to sight-read  rhythmic patterns
8.   Ability to   perform with accurate intonation
9.   Ability to   perform with suitable tone quality
10.   Ability to  perform with appropriate phrasing and expression
11.   Ability to  perform with appropriate tempo and style

The seven musical examples, transposed for the various instruments 
performed in this study, are presented in Appendix A.  Each musical 
example was selected following extended discussions with a number of 
musicians and music teachers concerning performance problems and after 
several trial examples were experimented with before the study was 
begun.
Tonal-Creative Examples 
Musical example one was utilized to evaluate students on Abilities 
1, 2, and 3.  This example was selected from several selections specially 
composed for the purpose.  The example used was considered most appro­
priate because of its lack of melodic and harmonic complexity, and for 
its potential in measuring the abilities to improvise a variation, to 
create a new ending, and to improvise a lower part.
Rhythmic Examples 
Musical examples two, three, four, and five were utilized to eval­
uate students on Abilities U, $, 6, and 7 respectively.  The investigator 
felt that an evaluation of rhythmic performance ability presented a most 
complex and perplexing problem.  However, after much experimentation, 
the writer decided to measure four distinct aspects of rhythm, and thus 
four distinct musical examples were finally selected.  Musical example 
two was designed to measure Ability i*, consistency of tempo.  This 
example utilizes a melodic line and is so composed as to expose any 
weaknesses attendant to rushing or dragging the tempo.  Musical example 
three was designed to evaluate Ability *&gt;&gt; rhythmic understanding.  It 
was theorized that if a student possessed an understanding of rhythmic

2U
principles he could successfully cope with the metrical changes found 
in this example.  A melodic line was purposely not included in order to 
minimize extraneous factors and emphasize the rhythmic aspect.  Musical 
example four was designed to measure Ability 6, comprehension of meter. 
This example was selected because it is so written that the meter may 
be changed from duple to triple in performance with a minimum of diffi­
culty, but yet contains rhythmic figures which would expose any hesita­
tion on the part of the performer.  Musical example five was designed to 
evaluate Ability 7» sight-reading.  Four short varying rhythmic patterns 
designed to evaluate students&#39; abilities to sight-read common rhythmic 
figures were selected to constitute this example.  There is ho melodic 
content, which minimizes the chance that the rhythmic aspect could be 
obscured or made unduly difficult.
Sensitivity Examples 
Musical examples six and seven were employed to evaluate Abilities 
8, 9, 10, and 11.  Musical example six was written with interval leaps 
designed to expose faulty intonation and was utilized in evaluating 
Ability 8, intonation.  This example also was employed to evaluate 
Ability 9, tone quality.  It was deemed suitable for this purpose since 
it does not require any rapid movement in performance.  Musical example 
seven was selected from several trial examples to be used for evaluating 
Ability 10, phrasing.  This example was also utilized for evaluating 
Ability 11, style.  Musical example seven was so designed that it could 
elicit responses indicative of students&#39;  sensitivity to phrasing and 
expression as well as sensitivity to suitable tempo and style.

25
The musical content of each of the examples was exactly the same 
for each instrument, although the examples were transposed for the 
various woodwind and brass instruments so that technical and range 
demands were comparable in difficulty for all instruments.  The examples 
were designed so that an advanced technique or unusual fluency was not 
required in performance.
Design and Analyses 
The six musical examples which were to be practiced by the students 
(the seventh was to be sight-read) were given to them two days before 
their performance of the examples was tape recorded.  The investigator 
carefully outlined the importance of the performance aspect of the study 
and stressed that each individual should attempt to achieve as artistic 
and sensitive a level of musical performance as possible.  Each student 
was verbally instructed how to prepare the examples but he was not given 
any technical help nor supervision.  The written instructions given to 
the students are presented in Appendix B.
The investigator supervised the tape recording of performances 
during January, February, and March, 1967.  Each student was allowed to 
play through the first example to orient himself to the acoustics of the 
room and to allow the investigator to set the sound level for the record­
ing.  All performances were recorded on the same Ampex tape recorder.
Only one student at a time was permitted in the recording room.
The eleven performance abilities were recorded by each student utilizing 
the six prepared musical examples and the sight-reading example, which 
was introduced at the recording session.  If a student hesitated during

26
the recording or seemed displeased with his performance of an example, 
he was allowed to repeat the example once.  One hundred and five of 
the original 123 subjects completed the performance aspect of this 
study.  Of the 18 students who did not participate in the performance 
evaluations, six were eliminated by the investigator because they dis­
played obvious anxiety or nervousness during the recording sessions; 12 
could not arrange a suitable audition time or were no longer enrolled 
in school for the second semester.

Rhythm Imagery
It can be seen from Table 6 that the Rhythm Imagery subtests, Tempo 
and Meter correlate higher with Ability 1 (Variation) and Ability 2 (New 
Ehding) than they do with any of the other nine abilities including the 
four Rhythmic Abilities.  However, scores for the Tempo test do correlate 
higher with Ability k (Tempo) and Ability 5 (Rhythm) than with any of 
the seven remaining abilities.  Conversely, the Meter test does not cor­
relate as high with Ability 6 (Meter) as was reasonably expected.  In 
general, scores on the Melody and Harmony tests demonstrate a stronger 
relationship to Rhythmic Abilities than do the Tempo and Meter tests. 
Scores for the Rhythm Imagery-Total test (Table 7) correlate higher 
with the combined Abilities 1-3 (Tonal-creative) than they do with the 
combined Abilities U-7  (Rhythmic) and they correlate lower with these 
latter combined abilities than do scores on the Tonal Imagery-Total 
test.
These results suggest a somewhat different pattern of relationships 
between MAP rhythm tests and Rhythmic Abilities from that found in the

Lincolnwood-Rochester study.  The correlations also deviate from the 
pattern that was hypothesized to hold.  One may only speculate on the 
factors which account for the contrast with previous results and the 
departure from those results which were expected.  Possible explana­
tions, of course, may be due to different characteristics of the groups 
used in the various studies or to that of sampling error.  All statis­
tical indices, such as correlation coefficients, are subject to sampling 
error which obscures the facts about a population.  It may be that the 
idiosyncracies of previous and present data make the inconsistencies 
appear greater than they really are.  No single study can provide 
crucial evidence on this possibility.
A second explanation for the inconsistencies involves the rating 
process.  The intercorrelations among the ratings (Table 9), considered 
in relation to the reliabilities of these ratings (Table 5)&gt; suggest 
that the judges were only moderately successful in assessing the unique 
aspects of separate abilities.  A large common factor does,  in fact, 
seem to be inherent in all of the performance evaluations.  Intrusion 
of one ability into the rating of a second could markedly alter the 
magnitude of the various correlations.  One might conjecture that more 
comprehensive training of the judges or a different set of musical 
examples would have made possible greater independence of ratings. 
However, both of these aspects seemed satisfactorily handled in the 
present study.
A third factor accounting for the findings may be associated with 
the previous and current musical training of the subjects.  To the ac­
complished musician, the eleven abilities may seem somewhat unique.  It

is possible, however, to conceive of a student with unusually high 
ability of one kind and rather low ability of another.  But there is 
a difference between a conceptual possibility and a demonstrated fact. 
Under certain kinds of musical instruction, specific potential differ­
ences among students may never clearly materialize.  For example, the 
high potential of Student A in rhythm, in&#39;contrast to that of Student 
B, may never be realized to any great extent.  Each student may mature 
musically at about the same pace in all aspects of his performance.
Thus, at any particular point in their careers, Student A may appear 
consistently superior to Student B in all aspects.  In effect, the sit­
uation may be analogous to that which is encountered ih intellectual 
skills such as reading and mathematical thinking.  There is some evi­
dence from elementary school achievement batteries that the relationship 
between these abilities does not decline from grade 3 to grade 8,  The 
students become more proficient in all abilities, but patterns of high 
and low ability do not become more sharply defined.  In music the same 
kind of overall progress in all facets of performance may occur.  The 
high relationships may not reflect weaknesses in the judges or the 
measuring instruments, but simply status of instructional procedures as 
it is reflected in student development.
Musical Sensitivity 
Scores on the Musical Sens it ivity-Phras ing test, as can be seen 
in Table 6, correlate highest with Ability 10 (Phrasing).  Conversely, 
these scores correlate lowest with each of the Rhythmic Abilities, 
Ability U (Tempo), Ability 5 (Rhythm), Ability 6 (Meter), and Ability 7

US
(Sight-reading).  Scores on the Musical Sensitivity-Balance test cor­
relate highest with Ability 10 (Phrasing) and Ability 11 (Style) and 
correlate comparably with Ability 1 (Variation), Ability 2  (New Ending), 
and Ability 3 (Lower Part).  For the Musical Sensitivity-Style test, 
scores correlate highest with Ability 10 (Phrasing) and Ability 11 
(Style).  Abilities 10 and 11 (Phrasing and Style, respectively) with 
which the Musical Sensitivity subtests correlate highest (and with which 
the Tonal Imagery subtests score similarly high), are generally musical 
behaviors and are not, as defined in this study, specific to instru­
mental music performance.  In regard to the combined relationships, it 
can be seen in Table 7 that the Musical Sens itivity-Total test corre­
lates highest with combined Abilities 8-11 (Sensitivity) but lower 
with these abilities than does the Tonal Imagery-Total test.
As hypothesized,  and in spite of unusually low reliabilities, a 
definite pattern of relationships between the Musical Sensitivity sub­
tests and sensitivity behaviors does exist.  The Phrasing and Style 
subtests correlate highest with musical sensitivity behaviors and the 
Balance test correlates comparatively high with three abilities related 
to musical sensitivity behaviors and with three abilities related to 
tonal and creative behaviors,  (it is interesting to note in Table 6, 
that of the five nonsignificant coefficients, four are associated with 
the Balance test.)  The Musical Sensitivity-Total test also correlates 
highest with musical sensitivity behaviors.  These findings are con­
sistent with those reported in the Lincolnwood-Rochester study.
MAP validity coefficients for the Composite test score, as re­
ported in Table 7, are .66 with Abilities 1-3 (Tonal-creative),  .53

with Abilities U—7 (Rhythmic),  .56 with Abilities 8-11 (Sensitivity), 
and .62 with all eleven combined abilities.  Comparatively, the cor­
relation of .62 between the composite test score and the total combined 
abilities is higher than the corresponding coefficient for general 
music abilities found in the Rochester study (.53) and lower than that 
found in the Lincolnwood study (.70).  It is curious to note that the 
correlation between the MAP composite score and all ratings combined 
was not the highest found in this study; the correlation between the 
MAP composite score and Abilities 1-3 (Tonal-creative) was .66.

CHAPTER V 
SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS
Problem of the Study
The purpose of this study was to gather information pertaining to 
the validity of the diagnostic use of MAP with instrumental music stu­
dents.  The specific problems of the study were to investigate:
1.  The relationships between MAP subtest scores and general 
musical behaviors of instrumental music students.
2.  The relationships between MAP subtest scores and musical 
behaviors specific to instrumental music students.
3.  The relationships between combined MAP subtest scores and 
overall musical behaviors of instrumental music students.
Design of the Study
MAP was administered to 10E&gt; members of the South Dakota State Uni­
versity Bands at Srookings.  These students were working toward bacca­
laureate degrees in various academic disciplines in the arts and 
sciences, and they constituted a cross-section of freshmen, sophomores, 
juniors, and seniors.
The students tape recorded their performances of seven specially 
composed musical examples, each designed to measure general musical abil­
ities and abilities related specifically to instrumental performance. 
Ratings of these recordings by qualified judges constituted the validity

h9
criteria.  The musical abilities which the musical examples were de­
signed to elicit were:
Ability  1:  Improvise a variation
Ability  2:  Create a new ending
Ability  3:  Improvise a lower part to a given melody
Ability  U:  Perform with consistent tempo
Ability  5&gt;s  Demonstrate rhythmic understanding
Ability  6:  Comprehend meter
Ability 7 s  Sight-read rhythmic patterns
Ability  8:  Perform   with  accurate intonation
Ability  9:  Perform with suitable tone quality
Ability 10:  Perform   with  appropriate phrasing and expression
Ability 11:  Perform   with  appropriate tempo and style
Six of the musical examples were practiced by the students  (the 
seventh was sight-read), and these were given to the students two days 
before their performances were tape recorded.  The evaluation of the 
students&#39; tape-recorded performances was independently accomplished by 
two judges— the writer and another staff member of the Music Department 
of South Dakota State Oliversity.  The judges rated the performance of 
each ability on a five-point rating scale.
After the students&#39; performances were evaluated, the ratings of 
the two judges for each of the eleven abilities for each student were 
summed in order to obtain a single rating score for each of the eleven 
abilities.  Next, points on subgroups of the abilities were summed to 
form three combined ratings and scores for all eleven abilities were 
added together to yield a composite rating.  Ratings for each of the 
eleven performance abilities were then intercorrelated with scores on 
each of the seven MAP subtests.  Similarly, the ratings for the three 
combined abilities and the composite performance rating-.were intercor­
related with the three MAP total test scores and the composite test 
score.

Split-halves reliability coefficients, corrected for length 
through the use of the Spearman-Brown Prophecy Formula, were obtained 
for each of the eleven MAP scores.  Reliability coefficients for the 
judges&#39; ratings of each of the eleven performance abilities, the com­
bined abilities, and the composite rating were also obtained.
Results
As hypothesized, the Tonal Imagery subtests were found to correlate 
highest with those abilities related to tonal and creative behaviors. 
Specifically, correlations for the Tonal Imagery-Melody test were .1*2 
with Ability 1 (Variation),  .57 with Ability 2 (New Ending), and .51 
with Ability 3 (Lower Part).  The Melody test also correlated relatively 
highly (.1*3) with Ability 8 (Intonation).  For the Tonal Imagery-Harmony 
test, correlations were .1*5 with Ability 1 (Variation), .55 with Ability 
2 (New Ending), and .50 with Ability 3 (Lower Part).  Similar to the 
Melody test, the Harmony test correlated .1*5 with Ability 8 (Intonation). 
In regard to combined abilities, the Tonal Imagery-Total test correlated 
highest (.62) with Abilities 1-3 (Tonal-creative) and with all eleven 
abilities,  ,58.
The relationships between tie Rhythm Imagery subtests and the 
Rhythmic Abilities were not as strong as were those between the Tonal 
Imagery tests and the Tonal Abilities.  That is, the Rhythmic Imagery- 
Tempo test correlated .36 with Ability 1* (Tempo),  .38 with Ability 5 
(Rhythm), .22 with Ability 6 (Meter), and .31 with Ability 7 (Sight- 
reading).  The correlations for the Rhythm Imagery-Meter test were .25 
with Ability 1* (Tempo),  .19 with Ability 5 (Rhythm),  .20 with Ability 6

(Meter), and .32 with Ability 7 (Sight-reading).  Overall, the corre­
lation between the Rhythm Imagery-Total test and the combined Abilities 
l*-8 (Rhythmic) was .39 and that with all eleven combined was .1*1*.  In 
contrast, the Tonal Imagery-Melody test correlated somewhat higher with 
Ability 1* (Tempo) and Ability 7 (Sight-reading) than did the Rhythm 
Imagery-Tempo test.  These coefficients were .1*0 for the former and .1*5 
for the latter.  Likewise, the Tonal Imagery-Harmony test correlated 
higher than did the Rhythm Imagery-Meter test with each of the four 
Rhythmic Abilities;  .1*2 with Ability 1* (Tempo),  .36 with Ability 5 
(Rhythm),  .25 with Ability 6 (Meter), and .1*6 with Ability 7 (Sight- 
reading) .  The correlation between the Tonal Imagery-Total test and the 
combined Abilities l*-7 (Rhythmic) was higher (.51) than that between 
the Rhythmic Imagery-Total test (.39) and these abilities.
As hypothesized, the Musical Sensitivity subtests correlated high­
est with the abilities related to sensitivity behaviors.  Specifically, 
the Musical Sensitivity-Phrasing test correlated .37 with Ability 8 
(Intonation),  .27 with Ability 9 (Tone Quality),  .21* with Ability 10 
(Phrasing), and .23 with Ability 11 (Style).  Similarly, coefficients 
found for the Musical S ens it ivity-Style test were .37 with Ability 8 
(Intonation),  .31* with Ability 9 (Tone Quality),  .1*3 with Ability 10 
(Phrasing), and .1*1 with Ability 11 (Style).  The Musical Sensitivity- 
Total test correlated highest (.1*7) with the combined Abilities 8-11 
(Sensitivity) and with all eleven abilities,  .1*6.
MAP composite test score correlations were .66 with combined 
Abilities 1-3 (Tonal-creative),  .53 with combined Abilities l*-7 
(Rhythmic),  .56 with combined Abilities 8-11 (Sensitivity), and .62

with the eleven abilities combined.
Conclusions
The findings of this study support the conclusion that MAP scores 
can serve as an objective aid to the music teacher in his efforts to 
adapt instruction to meet the individual needs and abilities of instru­
mental music students.  The Tonal Imagery scores can best serve to 
diagnose students&#39;  strengths and weaknesses in regard to tonal and crea­
tive aptitudes of a general nature, and those specific to instrumental 
performance as defined in this study.  The Musical Sensitivity results 
can serve a similar purpose with respect to the creative-expressive 
aptitudes.
The writer was unable to adequately interpret the results pertain­
ing to diagnostic validity of the Rhythm Imagery tests.  Therefore, it 
can only be suggested that, until further research is forthcoming, scores 
on both the Tonal Imagery and Rhythm Imagery total tests should be em­
ployed for identifying students with strengths and weaknesses in overall 
rhythmic aptitude.
Conclusions regarding specific methods and techniques for adapting 
instruction to individual students&#39; musical strengths and weaknesses 
were not in the domain of this study.  However, until more specific 
research findings are available, implications of this investigation may 
serve as a guide to teachers in exercising responsibility in these 
matters.
