### CHAPTER I

### INTRODUCTION

Every educator has their own way of teaching and conducting their classroom which often
creates a space where students feel comfortable and are able to be productive. Before educators
are allowed to *officially* teach, they must be certified within their state, have at least a bachelor’s
degree in their area of study, and also have a record of field training such as classroom
observations and student teaching. Once these requirements are met and they find a job within a
school district, they begin their journey as a fully certified teacher. On this journey, teachers are
continuously learning new information daily; not only through experience but through study as
well. They are not only teaching their students, but they are also learning how to present
information more effectively to better interest students and help them retain the information. It is
fair to say that everyone retains information differently at their own pace in their own way. The
same way that students are given information to learn, teachers have to learn as well when it
comes down to the overall structure that they desire for their classroom(s) as well as the daily
content that they’re teaching.

Thus, that is where the different approaches integrate as guides to help teachers better
their teaching strategies (on all grade levels). The different methods were specifically derived
from behavioral and cognitive psychology, and have appeared as roots of music education
research. Originally, these music learning theories were developed outside the field of music for
*psychological* reasons (GIML 2018). The ultimate goal of these practices were to “seek to
describe, explain, and possibly predict” musical behavior (Richard Colwell 2002).

---

Edward Gordon, Orff Schulwerk, and Zoltan Kodaly are three music educators who
developed a method/approach to teaching music to young children. These methods are a few of
the very first that came to be that caused a spark in the research of different ways that music
could be taught. Edward Gordon’s method focuses on the importance of learning sequences and
audiation. For example, a teacher could use a sound that is repetitive such as; a simple beat that
the class can clap. As they were clapping, the teacher could sing a short and simple melody
repetitively. This causes the students to remember the melody through repetition and sequence.
Orff Schulwerk’s Method focuses on the combination of  music, movement, drama, and speech
into lessons that are similar to child's play (primarily using percussion instruments). For
example, multiple students could be assigned to percussion instruments, dialogue, and some type
of dance. This is mainly targeted through classroom games or simple activities that we’ll discuss
later. Lastly, Zoltan Kodaly’s Method focuses on the use of hand signals during singing exercises
to provide a visual aid for vocal syllables.For example, there’s a term that most educators call
“solfege” that is used to describe the syllables of sound using our hands. Most people know it as
“do, re, mi, fa, sol, la, ti ,do”.

The “Kodaly Method” is most commonly used within schools in the State of Texas (TMEA
2007). Even though his theory has some valid points (which we will discuss later) My two
questions are, “Is this the best method of teaching?” and “Why are teachers within the State of
Texas required to teach the Kodaly Method?” (Taylor Walkup 1984)

### CHAPTER III EDWARD GORDON LEARNING THEORY

Edward Gordon researched music development for years before he developed his own
learning theory for music. He conducted experiments regarding audiation skills beginning with
infants from one month to eighteen months old, and began refining audiation skills in children
from eighteen months to age three. That research focused on investigating the levels of Music
Learning Theory, (stages and types of audiation, developmental and stabilized music aptitudes,
and rhythm in movement and music.) Based on extensive research and practical field testing by
Gordon and others, “Music Learning Theory has become a comprehensive method for teaching
audiation.” (GIML 2018) Which is Gordon’s term for the ability to think music in the mind with
understanding.

His theory includes principles that evolved into a guide for music teachers at any level and
teaching setting with a sequential order on how his theory should be taught (a learning
sequence.) He made the sequence in order to address specific curricular goals and allow those
goals to be in one accord with the instructor’s own teaching styles and beliefs. Overall, This was
to enhance the development of students’ tonal and rhythmic audiation. Through audiation,
students are able to draw greater meaning from the music they listen to, perform, improvise, and
compose. They are able to do this by identifying, creating ,and improvising unfamiliar patterns in
music of all genres/cultures. Music is another language in itself, so with audiation being the
foundation, (according to Gordon), his theory helps create stronger musicians. If we are not
strengthening our audiation skills daily (even as children) then it becomes very complex for us to
be able to grasp material and understand it as we get older.

strongly emphasize the fact that audiation is not directly  “imitation” nor “memorization”
(Terrance Bacon 2019).”

Pre-K/Kindergarten lesson, I applied Gordon’s theory by focusing on teaching the basics of the

### CHAPTER 1V ORFF SCHULWERK METHOD

The Orff Schulwerk Method, combines music, movement, drama, and speech into lessons
that are similar to a child playing on their own. It was developed by Carl Orff (1895–1982) and
his colleague Gunild Keetman(1904-1990) during the 1920’s in Germany. They both worked
tirelessly over the years to continue the development and spread of his teaching method. The
average person would wonder how the name for his teaching method came about “Orff
Schulwerk Method”. Of course the word “Orff” represented his last name and the term
"schulwerk" is German for "school work" or "schooling".

The Orff Approach was originally intended to teach music to children, but because of the
different benefits that it offers as far as coordination, dexterity, and concentration, the technique
is most often used to teach individuals with special needs. The simplicity of the technique allows
all ranges of special needs students to participate in the learning process. A big component of
this technique involves the use of percussion instruments. As discussed in Chapter 2, this is
where the exercising of one’s brain comes into place.

To elaborate, once this method came about, mentally handicapped students were now able
to easily perform tasks without the fear of being ridiculed or left behind inside and outside of the
classroom. When it comes to other students with special needs such as the visually
impaired/blind students, they tend to have "hesitant, jerky, and over controlled" movements.
These movements are caused by their breathing; very shallow and heavy breaths. In order to
achieve breath control, students were taught to use the different breathing and movement
exercises created by Carl Orff in order to relax their body and breathing. For the students with a hearing impairment, they were advised to use the Orff Approach by feeling the vibrations that
are created by the different percussion instruments. Since music is used as a form of healing,
students who have had injuries that have mentally impaired them can use the Orff Approach as a
form of therapy. Even elderly individuals who often become weak can use the Orff Approach to
help with memory, dexterity, and agility (Carl Orff 1925).

Orff had a very strong interest in percussion instruments. He considered the rhythm from
the percussion instruments as a natural, yet “basic” form of human expression. With this being
said, over time, Carl Orff came up with his own group of percussion instruments to help students
with special needs. This specific set of percussion instruments are often referred to as “Orff
Instruments” within the classroom. Music played on Orff instruments is often very simple and
easy to play even for first time musicians. Some of the instruments within the approach include
miniature xylophones, marimbas, glockenspiels, and metallophones; all of which have
removable bars (making it easier for students), resonating columns to project the sound, and they
can all be easily transported and stored.

Teachers who incorporate the Orff Approach also use different sized drums, and
non-pitched percussion instruments "to round out the songs that are sung and played". This
method began as just a practice, but grew into a guide for teachers over the years and is still
being used (mainly on a primary school/junior high) grade level. Even though the Orff approach
mainly focuses on percussion instruments, it also requires bodily percussion and voice such as
children singing, chanting, clapping, dancing, patting, and snapping their fingers along to
melodies and rhythms. The Orff Approach can be referred to as a "child-centered way of
learning" music education, but it treats music as a basic language and believes that “just as every child can learn language without formal instruction so can every child learn music by a gentle
and friendly approach.” This can also be known as  "Elemental Music making" because the
materials needed to teach students are "basic, natural, and close to a child’s world of thought and
fantasy". As stated before, in order for the Orff Approach to work effectively, teachers must
create an atmosphere that is similar to a “child’s world of play.” This allows participating
students to feel comfortable learning a new and often abstract musical skill, greater exploration
of their skills and abilities, and keeps them from feeling *evaluated* or *judged* by their peers and
teachers. Any student participating in an “Orff-based” classroom (or any classroom) should not
feel the pressure of performing(Carl Orff 1920). Nervousness is very normal, but coming from
that of a special needs student, they should not be anxious due to their needs because every
student in an “Orff- based” classroom is treated as an equal, even when performing a solo.

clear models and basic processes that all creative teachers use to guide their organization of
musical ideas.

### CHAPTER V

### ZOLTAN KODALY METHOD

The Kodály method is an approach to music education based on the idea that music
should be a “social” and “cultural” experience. The Kodály Method puts forth the idea that
musical concepts, creativity, and collaboration are “best taught” in group music lessons. This
method, (similar to Orff and Gordon) is aimed towards young children but can be experienced in
adults as well. According to Kodaly, “music teachers should emphasize musical material that is
connected to their students' culture and heritage (Zoltan Kodaly 1882-1967).” Zoltán Kodály
(1882-1967) officially developed the Kodály method in Hungary in the first half of the twentieth
century.

As Kodaly grew older, music was a subject that was frequently taught in one-on-one
instruction (private lessons). During the time that he was a student studying music, emphasis of
works from composers like Bach, Mozart, Haydn, and Beethoven were prevalent (these
composers were also German as well). While studying these composers and their works, Kodály
slowly but surely began to see the true value of teaching musicianship through folk music that
students were very familiar with at that time. He felt that he could teach musical literacy and ear
training through “sight-singing, solfège, and lyrics from his students.”

Over the years, as Kodály developed his teaching methods, he was stuck on a specific
approach. This approach was described as “social, yet kinesthetic”. He believed that it could
work on both an elementary school level and a collegiate level. In 1945, Kodály's methods
*officially* became part of the curriculum for Hungarian schools (elementary). This led to the
Kodály method being spread rapidly over the following decade. Ever since then,  Kodály’s approach to teaching music has gained “international recognition and won adherents around the
world.”

Music teachers today typically use the Kodály method in classrooms, where groups of
students learn foundational musical elements and rhythmic patterns. The initial goal of Kodály’s
method was to give students a first-hand experience of music and to never make it seem like a
“dry” academic exercise (Zoltan Kodaly 1882-1967). On a collegiate level, more advanced
topics like syncopation, counterpoint, and improvisation are now more prevalent within the state
of Texas and nationwide (using the Kodaly method of course). On his journey, Kodály did end
up developing exercises to help music teachers design a “Kodály curriculum” for their own
students. In order for this method to be as effective as possible, proper instruction of the Kodály
method must be given. It involves the adherence to a series of key principles listed below:

**Learning by singing:** According to Kodály, the human voice is the fundamental instrument, and
it should be essential to musical training. Students should also be able to”gain musical literacy
through sung solfège” using a moveable-do system. **Hand signs:** Solfège and sight-singing can
be supplemented by hand signs. **Rhythmic proficiency:** The sight-reading of rhythmic patterns
(including whole notes, half notes, quarter notes, eighth notes, sixteenth notes, and various
tuplets). **Collaboration:** Kodály believed that creativity and collaboration can be brought out in
group music lessons. He also stated that “Music students should collaborate with one another in
exercises ranging from clapping to choral singing to instrumental accompaniment.” **Cultural
connections:** He believed that music instructors should emphasize folk music (even pop songs)
in a way for students to understand to create an inner connection to music.

It is fair to say that the Kodály method has similarities with other styles of music education
developed by Carl Orff and Edward Gordon but it still has its differences. One of the biggest
differences being the fact that Kodaly and Gordon did not aim their methods specifically towards
special needs students like Orff. This isn’t to say that special needs students can’t use their
methods, but they weren’t *specifically* designed for them. Kodály, Gordon, and Orff  all sought to
teach music in a social, yet “exploratory”way but they all decided to still take a different
approach from all aspects. Kodály’s method has been notorious for emphasizing existing pieces
of knowledge from the average student and this has caused music students all over the world to
get a chance to simply apply their prior knowledge to their independent practices and strengthen
their skills through education and performance.

The state of Texas requires teachers to use the Kodaly method within their classrooms
because it has proven to produce higher effectiveness within primary school students (Taylor

Walkup 1984). The overall curriculum for most school districts (within the state of Texas)
combines a strong basis in Kodály tradition and “time-tested” methodology. Research has proven
that “research-based” innovation and openness from the Kodaly method creates the most
effective new techniques and tools for primary classrooms (including those found in other
approaches).

This is not to say that most primary school teachers are limited to just the Kodaly method,
but they are required to apply some of Kodaly’s methodology within their lesson plans (Taylor
Walkup 1984). Knowing how to apply and use solfege (Kodaly’s method) is very important as a
musician because Solfege is great for *identifying* relationships between different notes in music.
If a musician/vocalist is not able to tell the difference between notes and pitches within music,
then their future career is most definitely at risk. Speaking from personal experience, the Kodaly
method was first introduced to me in the 3rd Grade. At the time, singing was my passion and I
vaguely remember being taught the importance of pitch and tone using solfege before we were
taught anything else. At the time, I didn’t really understand why we were using solfege, I just
thought that it was part of the daily warm-up, but as I got older and began studying music more
closely, I realized that solfege sets the foundation for producing the proper sound whether you
are singing, playing piano, or any instrument of your choice.

Most vocalists and musicians to this day have a routine of “warming-up” before practice or
a performance. This is a routine that was taught to us at a very young age. A warm-up can be
described as “a series of exercises meant to prepare the voice for singing, acting, or other use
(DeFatta Rima 2015).” Even on a collegiate and professional level, warm-ups are constantly
being utilized before every performance. Unknowingly, professional musicians and collegiate musicians use Kodaly’s method during this process. Moving up and down a musical scale and
properly matching pitch is what Kodaly’s method is all about, so it’s fair to say that this method
is not only used nationally but this is why the state of Texas has made it a requirement for this
method to be taught (initially developing stronger and effective musicians) .

Within my collegiate career studying music education, I was required to do classroom
observations locally. During my observations, I took note that the Kodaly method is not only
very entertaining for primary students, but it truly does keep them engaged and interested.
During one of my observations at a primary school in HISD (Houston Independent School
District), I noticed that there were some students who truly were trying to get the method down
and make sure that they were matching pitch with their teacher (even if that meant projecting
their voices over their other classmates). It was very intriguing to physically see how much of an
effect that this method has on children and adults. Even the teacher seemed to be very indulged
in making sure that her students were physically getting the hand signs correct, and didn’t move
on with the next lesson if the entire class was not vocally and physically correct.

The Kodaly method is very well respected within the state of Texas to the point where it has
also been added to the curriculum on a collegiate level. Throughout the duration of my time
studying music education in college, learning the Kodaly method, and learning how to apply it
within my future career as a teacher has been a requirement. Learning all the theories discussed
thus far was also a requirement. Not only was it a requirement, but it’s also still being used
within collegiate music classrooms as a means of teaching future music educators and current
ensembles.
