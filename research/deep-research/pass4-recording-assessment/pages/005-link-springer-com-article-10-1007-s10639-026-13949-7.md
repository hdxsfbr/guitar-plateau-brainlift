Bridging minds and machines: a comparative study of AI and human rater agreement and reliability in educational assessment

[Download PDF](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7.pdf)

Abstract
--------

Building on cognitive theories, this study investigates interrater reliability (IRR) and interrater agreement (IRA) between human raters and artificial intelligence (AI) models (ChatGPT-4 and Gemini) in scoring open-ended (constructed response) and single best answer questions. The study involved 73 third-year undergraduate students from the Department of Primary School Education, enrolled in the Measurement and Evaluation course. Of these, 46 students answered the open-ended question, and 69 answered the single best answer question. The analysis focused on comparing IRR and IRA between human raters and AI models, as well as between the AI models themselves. Results indicated that AI models demonstrated higher IRR and IRA than human raters for the open-ended question, characterized by less structured responses and multidimensional scoring criteria, while human raters performed slightly better for the single best answer question. AI models showed relatively consistent scoring performance across task types, whereas human raters exhibited greater variability, particularly for the open-ended task. These findings highlight task-dependent differences in scoring consistency that are interpretable through cognitive theories, contributing to ongoing debates about the appropriate role of AI in educational assessment.

1 Introduction
--------------

Interrater reliability (IRR) refers to the extent to which the scores assigned by different raters maintain a consistent proportional relationship, particularly when those scores are adjusted to account for each rater’s average scoring tendencies (Tinsley & Weiss, [1975](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR46 "Tinsley, H. E., & Weiss, D. J. (1975). Interrater reliability and agreement of subjective judgments. Journal of Counseling Psychology, 22(4), 358–376. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1037/h0076640
                  
                ")
). IRR differs from interrater agreement (IRA), which focuses on the degree to which raters provide identical scores for the same tasks or individuals (Gisev et al., [2013](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR14 "Gisev, N., Bell, J. S., & Chen, T. F. (2013). Interrater agreement and interrater reliability: key concepts, approaches, and applications. Research in social & administrative pharmacy: RSAP, 9(3), 330–338. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sapharm.2012.04.004
                  
                ")
). Estimates of IRR assess whether raters rank targets in a manner that is relatively consistent with one another, focusing on the equivalence of relative rankings rather than absolute scores. In contrast, IRA evaluates the degree of absolute consensus among raters’ scores, addressing whether the ratings provided are interchangeable or equivalent in their absolute values (LeBreton & Senter, [2008](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR25 "LeBreton, J. M., & Senter, J. L. (2008). Answers to 20 questions about interrater reliability and interrater agreement. Organizational Research Methods, 11(4), 815–852. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1177/1094428106296642
                  
                ")
).

While both concepts are crucial for determining the credibility of measurement processes (Kottner et al., [2011](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR23 "Kottner, J., Audigé, L., Brorson, S., Donner, A., Gajewski, B. J., Hróbjartsson, A., Roberts, C., Shoukri, M., & Streiner, D. L. (2011). Guidelines for reporting reliability and agreement studies (GRRAS) were proposed. Journal of Clinical Epidemiology, 64(1), 96–106. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclinepi.2010.03.002
                  
                ")
), challenges arise due to rater effects, including systematic biases and differential rater functioning over time, where raters’ performance shifts as they engage with a scoring task (Gwet, [2014](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR15 "Gwet, K. L. (2014). Handbook of inter-rater reliability: The definitive guide to measuring the extent of agreement among raters. Advanced Analytics, LLC.")
). These effects can distort score validity, introducing construct-irrelevant variance that undermines fairness in assessments (Wind & Peterson, [2018](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR52 "Wind, S. A., & Peterson, M. E. (2018). A systematic review of methods for evaluating rating quality in language assessment. Language Testing, 35(2), 161–192. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1177/0265532216686999
                  
                ")
). The stakes are particularly high in contexts like academic placements, certifications, or policy formulations, where assessment outcomes directly influence critical decisions (Kane, [2013](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR20 "Kane, M. T. (2013). Validating the interpretations and uses of test scores. Journal of Educational Measurement, 50(1), 1–73. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1111/jedm.12000
                  
                ")
).

Researchers have employed multiple strategies to address rater variability and disagreement, including statistical modeling to detect and control for rater effects (Myford & Wolfe, [2004](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR33 "Myford, C. M., & Wolfe, E. W. (2004). Detecting and measuring rater effects using many-facet Rasch measurement: Part II. Journal of Applied Measurement, 5(2), 189–227.")
), structured training programs to enhance scoring consistency (Wendler et al., [2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR50 "Wendler, C., Glazer, N., & Cline, F. (2019). Examining the calibration process for raters of the GRE® General Test. ETS Research Report Series, 1-19. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1002/ets2.12245
                  
                ")
), using scoring rubrics to guide raters (Reddy & Andrade, [2009](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR39 "Reddy, Y. M., & Andrade, H. (2009). A review of rubric use in higher education. Assessment.")
), and technological innovations such as automated scoring systems to supplement human evaluations (Attali & Burstein, [2006](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR1 "Attali, Y., & Burstein, J. (2006). Automated essay scoring with e-rater® V.2. The Journal of Technology, Learning and Assessment, 4(3).")
). Despite these advances, achieving consistently high IRR and IRA remains elusive (Raczynski et al., [2015](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR36 "Raczynski, K. R., Cohen, A. S., Engelhard, G., & Lu, Z. (2015). Comparing the effectiveness of self-paced and collaborative frame-of-reference training on rater accuracy in a large-scale writing assessment: Comparing rater training methods. Journal of Educational Measurement, 52(3), 301–318. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1111/jedm.12079
                  
                ")
). Human raters inherently bring individual biases, idiosyncratic cognitive processes, and subjective interpretations to scoring tasks, resulting in variability and potential inconsistencies (Wind, [2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR51 "Wind, S. A. (2019). Examining the impacts of rater effects in performance Assessments. Applied psychological measurement, 43(2), 159–171. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1177/0146621618789391
                  
                ")
).

One key factor that has been proposed to influence raters’ performance is the cognitive load associated with different question types. Prior research suggests that the cognitive demands placed on raters vary between single best answer (SBA) questions and open-ended questions (OE) (Sychev et al., [2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR44 "Sychev, O., Anikin, A., & Prokudin, A. (2020). Automatic grading and hinting in open-ended text questions. Cognitive Systems Research, 59, 264–272. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cogsys.2019.09.025
                  
                ")
), and that such differences are associated with variations in interrater agreement and reliability (Raz et al., [2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR38 "Raz, T., Luchini, S., Beaty, R., & Kenett, Y. (2024). Bridging the measurement gap: A large language model method of assessing open-ended question complexity. Proceedings of the Annual Meeting of the Cognitive Science Society, 46. Retrieved from https://escholarship.org/uc/item/5k68s597")
). OE questions require learners to comprehend, analyze, and synthesize information, often producing longer and more complex responses (Kaya Uyanık & Ertuna, [2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR21 "Kaya Uyanik, G., & Ertuna, L. (2022). Examination of testlet effect in open-ended items. Sage Open, 12(1). 
                  https://doi.org/10.1177/21582440221079849
                  
                ")
). As a result, raters may need to engage in higher-order cognitive processes, such as interpreting arguments, evaluating coherence, and applying multi-dimensional rubrics (Lee et al., [2012](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR26 "Lee, Y., Kinzie, M. B., & Whittaker, J. V. (2012). Impact of online support for teachers’ open-ended questioning in preK science activities. Teaching and Teacher Education, 28, 568–577. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tate.2012.01.002
                  
                ")
). Prior studies suggest that these task characteristics are linked to greater variability in scoring and, consequently, lower IRA and IRR (McMillan, [2017](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR31 "McMillan, J. H. (2017). Classroom assessment: Principles and practice that enhance student learning and motivation. Pearson.")
). By contrast, SBA questions involve more constrained judgments of correctness and are generally considered to place lower cognitive demands on raters, which has been associated with reduced disagreement and higher consistency (Martinez, [1999](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR30 "Martinez, M. E. (1999). Cognition and the question of test item format. Educational Psychologist, 34(4), 207–218. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1207/s15326985ep3404_2
                  
                ")
). From this perspective, assessment task type is theorized to influence the conditions under which scoring consistency and reliability are more or less likely to emerge, with SBA questions often demonstrating higher IRA and IRR than OE constructed response questions.

Dual Process Theory (Evans & Stanovich, [2013](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR9 "Evans, J. S., & Stanovich, K. E. (2013). Dual-process theories of higher cognition: Advancing the debate. Perspectives on Psychological Science, 8, 223–241. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691612460685
                  
                ")
) and Cognitive Load Theory (Sweller, [1988](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR42 "Sweller, J. (1988). Cognitive load during problem solving: Effects on learning. Cognitive Science, 12(2), 257–285. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog1202_4
                  
                ")
) provide complementary theoretical frameworks for interpreting potential sources of rater variability, further enriched by insights from Cognitive Bias Theory (Tversky & Kahneman, [1974](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR48 "Tversky, A., & Kahneman, D. (1974). Judgment under uncertainty: Heuristics and biases. Science, 185(4157), 1124–1131. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1126/science.185.4157.1124
                  
                ")
). Dual Process Theory proposes that human cognition operates through two modes of processing: System 1, characterized as fast, automatic, and intuitive, and System 2, which is slower, effortful, and analytical (Evans, [2008](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR8 "Evans, J. S. (2008). Dual-processing accounts of reasoning, judgment, and social cognition. Annual Review of Psychology, 59, 255–278. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.psych.59.103006.093629
                  
                ")
). From a theoretical perspective, rater judgments may rely more heavily on one system or the other depending on task characteristics and contextual demands. For relatively simple or routine judgments, System 1 processing is theorized to support efficient decision-making through heuristic cues, whereas more complex or ambiguous tasks are theorized to require greater engagement of System 2 to apply scoring criteria systematically and reduce error (Vanhove et al., [2016](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR49 "Vanhove, A. J., Gibbons, A. M., & Kedharnath, U. (2016). Rater agreement, accuracy, and experienced cognitive load: Comparison of distributional and traditional assessment approaches to rating performance. Human Performance, 29(5), 378–393. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1080/08959285.2016.1192632
                  
                ")
).

Importantly, prior theoretical work suggests that under conditions commonly associated with cognitive strain—such as time pressure or fatigue—individuals may be more likely to rely on intuitive processing, which may increase variability in judgments that require integrative evaluation (Evans, [2008](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR8 "Evans, J. S. (2008). Dual-processing accounts of reasoning, judgment, and social cognition. Annual Review of Psychology, 59, 255–278. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.psych.59.103006.093629
                  
                ")
). However, the present study does not directly assess these cognitive processes and draws on Dual Process Theory solely as an interpretive lens.

Cognitive Load Theory (Sweller, [1988](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR42 "Sweller, J. (1988). Cognitive load during problem solving: Effects on learning. Cognitive Science, 12(2), 257–285. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog1202_4
                  
                ")
) complements this perspective by emphasizing the finite nature of cognitive resources and the role of task design in shaping processing demands. The theory distinguishes between intrinsic cognitive load, arising from task complexity, and extraneous cognitive load, which may stem from factors such as rubric design or clarity of evaluation criteria (Paas et al., [2003](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR35 "Paas, F., Tuovinen, J. E., Tabbers, H., & van Gerven, P. (2003). W. M. Cognitive load measurement as a means to advance cognitive load theory. Educational Psychologist, 38(1), 63–71. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1207/S15326985EP3801_8
                  
                ")
). In theory, when task demands exceed available cognitive resources, systematic processing may become more difficult, potentially increasing reliance on heuristic strategies (Sweller et al., [2011](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR43 "Sweller, J., Ayres, P., & Kalyuga, S. (2011). Cognitive load theory. Springer.")
). This framework is frequently used in prior research to interpret why complex, multi-dimensional scoring tasks are often associated with greater variability in rater judgments.

Cognitive Bias Theory (Tversky & Kahneman, [1974](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR48 "Tversky, A., & Kahneman, D. (1974). Judgment under uncertainty: Heuristics and biases. Science, 185(4157), 1124–1131. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1126/science.185.4157.1124
                  
                ")
) further extends this interpretive framework by identifying systematic tendencies that may influence judgment under demanding conditions. For example, anchoring, confirmation, leniency, or severity biases have been theorized to affect rater decisions, particularly in contexts requiring sustained attention or complex evaluation (Feldman, [1981](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR10 "Feldman, J. M. (1981). Beyond attribution theory. Cognitive processes in performance appraisal. Journal of Applied Psychology, 66(2), 127–148. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.66.2.127
                  
                ")
; Nickerson, [1998](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR34 "Nickerson, R. S. (1998). Confirmation bias: A ubiquitous phenomenon in many guises. Review.")
). Prior literature suggests that such biases may be more likely to emerge when cognitive resources are taxed, encouraging reliance on intuitive or heuristic-based judgments (Gigerenzer & Brighton, [2009](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR13 "Gigerenzer, G., & Brighton, H. (2009). Homo heuristicus. why biased minds make better inferences. Topics in Cognitive Science, 1(1), 107–143. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1756-8765.2008.01006.x
                  
                ")
). As with the other theoretical perspectives discussed, these concepts are used in the present study to contextualize observed scoring patterns rather than to make claims about directly measured cognitive states.

Taken together, these theoretical perspectives highlight enduring challenges in human scoring, particularly the susceptibility of rater judgments to variability and bias under cognitively demanding conditions. Although these limitations are well documented, they also point to the need for assessment approaches that can support consistency while reducing the influence of such rater effects. In this context, increasing attention has turned to the potential role of generative artificial intelligence (AI) in educational assessment (Bui & Barrot, [2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR4 "Bui, N. M., & Barrot, J. S. (2024). ChatGPT as an automated essay scoring tool in the writing classrooms: how it compares with human scoring. Education and Information Technologies. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1007/s10639-024-12891-w
                  
                ")
; Kortemeyer, [2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR22 "Kortemeyer, G. (2024). Performance of the pre-trained large language model GPT-4 on automated short answer grading. Discover Artificial Intelligence, 4(47).
                  https://doi.org/10.1007/s44163-024-00147-y
                  
                ")
). AI is defined as systems that mimic cognitive abilities commonly associated with human characteristics such as learning, speech, and problem solving (Dwivedi et al., [2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR7 "Dwivedi, Y. K. (2021). Artificial intelligence (AI): Multidisciplinary perspectives on emerging challenges, opportunities, and agenda for research, practice and policy. International Journal of Information Management, 57.  
                  https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijinfomgt.2019.08.002
                  
                ")
). AI systems are designed to follow predefined algorithms and models that involve systematic decision-making processes based on data inputs (Mizumoto & Eguchi, [2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR32 "Mizumoto, A., & Eguchi, M. (2023). Exploring the potential of using an AI language model for automated essay scoring. Research Methods in Applied Linguistics, 2(2), 1–13. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1016/j.rmal.2023.100050
                  
                ")
). AI’s performance remains unaffected by cognitive limitations, allowing it to continuously apply the same analytical criteria (Xiao et al., [2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR54 "Xiao, C., Ma, W., Song, Q., Xu, S. X., Zhang, K., Wang, Y., & Fu, Q. (2024). Human-AI collaborative essay scoring: A dual-process framework with LLMs. arXiv Preprint.ttps://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2401.06431")
). In principle, AI applies the programmed criteria consistently. However, the quality of this consistency depends heavily on the training data, the robustness of the model, and its alignment with the intended scoring rubrics or criteria (Bansal et al., [2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR2 "Bansal, C., Pandey, K. K., Goel, R., Sharma, A., & Jangirala, S. (2023). Artificial intelligence (Ai) bias impacts: Classification framework for effective mitigation. Issues in Information Systems, 24(4), 367–389. 
                  https://doi.org/10.48009/4_iis_2023_128
                  
                ")
). If the model is trained on biased or insufficient data, its consistent application of these criteria may still result in systematic errors or unintended biases (Rinta-Kahila et al., [2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR40 "Rinta-Kahila, T., Someh, I., Gillespie, N., Indulska, M., & Gregor, S. (2021). Algorithmic decision-making and system destructiveness: A case of automatic debt recovery. European Journal of Information Systems, 31(3), 313–338. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1080/0960085X.2021.1960905
                  
                ")
).

The growing use of AI in educational assessment has spurred interest in its potential to enhance grading efficiency and accuracy. However, studies examining the performance of AI systems in scoring student work presented conflicting results. Teckwani et al. ([2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR45 "Teckwani, S. H., Wong, A. H. W., Luke, V., N., & Low, I. (2024). Accuracy and reliability of large language models in assessing learning outcomes achievement across cognitive domains. Advances in Physiology Education, 48, 904–914. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1152/advan.00137.2024
                  
                ")
) found that large language models like GPT-3.5, GPT-4, and Gemini, while competent, did not meet the assessment standards of human graders, showing lower IRR in comparison to human-human grading. Similarly, Bui and Barrot ([2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR4 "Bui, N. M., & Barrot, J. S. (2024). ChatGPT as an automated essay scoring tool in the writing classrooms: how it compares with human scoring. Education and Information Technologies. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1007/s10639-024-12891-w
                  
                ")
) observed weak to moderate correlations between ChatGPT’s scores and those of experienced human raters, with low consistency in repeated assessments. Other studies, such as Mizumoto and Eguchi ([2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR32 "Mizumoto, A., & Eguchi, M. (2023). Exploring the potential of using an AI language model for automated essay scoring. Research Methods in Applied Linguistics, 2(2), 1–13. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1016/j.rmal.2023.100050
                  
                ")
), highlight that while AI systems like GPT can offer reliable scoring, their accuracy is influenced by linguistic features and model updates, suggesting a need for more context-specific training. Moreover, Li et al. ([2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR28 "Li, J., Jangamreddy, N. K., Hisamoto, R., ., Bhansali, R., Dyda, A., ., Zaphir, L., & Glencross, M. (2024). AI-assisted marking: Functionality and limitations of ChatGPT in written assessment evaluation. Australasian Journal of Educational Technology, 40(4), 56–72. 
                  https://doi.org/10.14742/ajet.9463
                  
                ")
) demonstrated that while ChatGPT showed high accuracy for higher quality assessments, its performance varied significantly with different prompts. These inconsistencies emphasize the need for further research to enhance the reliability of AI in assessment contexts. Additionally, the majority of studies on the effectivess of AI in scoring have focused on English-language assessments, yet AI is increasingly being used to evaluate assignments in multiple languages (Horbach et al., [2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR17 "Horbach, A., Pehlke, J., Laarmann-Quante, R., & Ding, Y. (2024). Crosslingual content scoring in five languages using machine-translation and multilingual transformer models. International Journal of Artificial Intelligence in Education, 34, 1294–1320. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1007/s40593-023-00370-1
                  
                ")
). Given the growing diversity of linguistic contexts in which AI-based assessment tools are deployed, there is a need for more research in languages beyond English to test the effectiveness of AI systems in global educational settings. This will allow the development of cross-linguistic models capable of providing accurate, consistent, and equitable evaluations across various languages and contexts.

Considering the ongoing challenges in achieving high IRR and IRA due to rater effects, as well as the mixed findings regarding the effectiveness of AI in assessment, this study aims to compare IRR and IRA across human raters, ChatGPT-4, and Gemini—two widely used AI models. The assessments were conducted in Turkish, addressing a gap in AI research that has predominantly focused on English-language contexts (Horbach et al., [2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR17 "Horbach, A., Pehlke, J., Laarmann-Quante, R., & Ding, Y. (2024). Crosslingual content scoring in five languages using machine-translation and multilingual transformer models. International Journal of Artificial Intelligence in Education, 34, 1294–1320. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1007/s40593-023-00370-1
                  
                ")
). The study examines how human raters and AI models differ in scoring both SBA and OE questions. In addition, reliability and agreement between the two AI models were analyzed to explore whether differences in model architecture are associated with variations in assessment outcomes. The study seeks to evaluate whether AI tools can support scoring consistency when used alongside human raters, potentially helping to mitigate some sources of variability in grading. Ultimately, the study contributes to the broader discussion on the role of AI in educational assessment, particularly with respect to its reliability and appropriate integration into assessment practices.

In particular, the study seeks answers to the following questions:

1.  1.
    
    How do IRA and IRR differ across various rater pairs, including human raters, AI models, and mixed rater pairs, when assessing OE items?
    
2.  2.
    
    How do human raters, AI models, and mixed rater pairs compare in terms of IRA and IRR when assessing SBA questions?
    
3.  3.
    
    Are there statistically significant differences in scoring means among raters, and how do these differences vary between OE and SBA formats?
    

Drawing on Dual Process Theory, Cognitive Load Theory, and Cognitive Bias Theory as interpretive frameworks, we hypothesize that differences in task structure between OE and SBA questions will be associated with variability in human raters’ scoring performance (Martinez, [1999](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR30 "Martinez, M. E. (1999). Cognition and the question of test item format. Educational Psychologist, 34(4), 207–218. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1207/s15326985ep3404_2
                  
                ")
). OE items involve less constrained responses and multidimensional evaluation criteria, which prior literature links to greater variability in the application of scoring criteria and lower consistency in scoring outcomes. In contrast, AI models apply scoring criteria in a uniform and repeatable manner and are not subject to fluctuations in attention, fatigue, or time pressure. Accordingly, we expect automated models to demonstrate relatively more stable scoring performance across task types, particularly for the OE item characterized by greater response variability.

We further hypothesize that both human raters and automated models will exhibit higher reliability and agreement when scoring the SBA question. SBA items are highly structured and rule-based, which prior research associates with reduced interpretive ambiguity and more consistent scoring (Martinez, [1999](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR30 "Martinez, M. E. (1999). Cognition and the question of test item format. Educational Psychologist, 34(4), 207–218. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1207/s15326985ep3404_2
                  
                ")
). As such, greater IRA and IRR are expected across rater types for the SBA item, reflecting alignment between task structure and scoring demands rather than differences in cognitive load per se.

It should be noted that the present study focuses specifically on scoring consistency, operationalized through IRR and IRA, rather than on the full validation of score interpretations. While reliability is a necessary condition for valid assessment, it is not sufficient on its own to establish that scores accurately reflect the quality or depth of student learning. Accordingly, the aim of this study is to examine how consistently human and automated raters apply shared scoring criteria across different task types, thereby providing foundational evidence relevant to, but not conclusive of assessment validity.

2 Methods
---------

### 2.1 Working group

The study participants comprised 73 third-year undergraduate students (prospective teachers) enrolled in the Department of Primary School Education, who participated in the midterm examination of the Measurement and Evaluation course in education. Among the participants, 21 were male and 52 were female. However, response rates varied across question types. Specifically, 46 students answered the OE question, while 69 students responded to the SBA question. Students who left the OE question blank (27 out of 73) or did not respond to the SBA question (4 out of 73) were excluded from the analysis.

Given these variations in sample size across item types and the involvement of multiple raters, post hoc power analyses were conducted to evaluate whether the available data were sufficient. These analyses were performed using the calculateIccPower() function from the ICC.Sample.Size v.1.0 package (Rathbone et al., [2015](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR37 "Rathbone, A., Saurabh, S., & Kumbhare, D. (2015). ICC.Sample.Size: Calculation of sample size and power for ICC (version 1.0) [R Package].")
), employing a two-sided hypothesis test with a significance level of α = 0.05. The cutoff value for statistical power was set at 0.80, in line with the commonly used threshold recommended by Cohen ([1988](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR5 "Cohen, J. (1988). Statistical power analysis for the behavioral sciences (2nd ed.). Lawrence Erlbaum Associates.")
). For the SBA item, with a sample size of 69 and four raters (two human raters and two automated raters), the observed IRR of ICC = 0.76 yielded a statistical power of 1.00, exceeding the commonly accepted cutoff value of 0.80. This indicates excellent power and suggests that the sample size and rater configuration were more than sufficient to reliably detect a high level of agreement. For the OE item, given a sample size of 46 and the same number of raters, an overall ICC of 0.25 resulted in a power value of 0.93, which also surpasses the 0.80 threshold. Although the magnitude of agreement for the OE items was relatively low, the power analysis demonstrates that the study was adequately powered to detect this level of interrater consistency. Overall, these findings indicate a low risk of Type II error for both item types, supporting the adequacy of the sample sizes and rater structure for evaluating IRR and IRA in the present study.

### 2.2 Measurement and evaluation course

The Measurement and Evaluation course in education teaches students how to assess and analyze various aspects of the educational process, focusing on the development, implementation, and interpretation of assessment tools and techniques. It covers key concepts such as the design of valid and reliable assessments, the distinction between different types of variables (e.g., dependent, independent), and the application of statistical methods to evaluate student performance. Students learn to critically examine measurement principles in the context of educational settings, considering the implications of factors like school climate, student diversity, and teacher effectiveness. The course also emphasizes the importance of aligning assessments with educational goals, establishing fairness, and applying both formative and summative evaluation strategies. Through this course, future educators gain the skills necessary to assess learning outcomes, provide constructive feedback, and improve educational practices based on data-driven insights.

### 2.3 Exam questions

#### 2.3.1 OE constructed response question

**_“_**You have recently started teaching at a rural primary school where you are the sole educator. While the school’s infrastructure is adequate, your students face significant challenges that hinder their overall development:

*   Resource Limitations: Insufficient books, writing materials, and resources for art, music, and physical education.

From the perspective of measurement and evaluation, outline how you would address these issues. Your response should include:

1.  1.

2.  2.

3.  3.

4.  4.
    
    Outcome Review: Explain how you would measure the effectiveness of your actions and critically evaluate their outcomes.
    

Your response will be assessed based on:

*   Understanding of Measurement and Evaluation: Clear differentiation and application of the two concepts.

*   Clarity and Organization: Well-structured, coherent, and clear presentation of ideas._”_
    

The given question aimed to measure several cognitive processes from Bloom’s Taxonomy ([1956](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR3 "Bloom, B. S. (1956). Taxonomy of educational objectives, handbook: The cognitive domain. David McKay.")
), including application, analysis, synthesis, and evaluation. It required the student to apply theoretical knowledge of measurement and evaluation to a real-world classroom scenario, breaking down problems like resource shortages and hygiene issues. The student had to analyze these issues, prioritize interventions, and creatively devise solutions aligned with measurement and evaluation principles. Additionally, to examine whether the question measured the intended traits, feedback was sought from two professors specializing in educational measurement and evaluation.

#### 2.3.2 SBA question

For the SBA item, the correct response structure was defined a priori based on established principles of educational research methodology. The identification of dependent and independent variables, variable classifications, and distinctions between control and confounding variables followed standard, widely accepted methodological conventions. To warrant content validity and accuracy, the expected correct responses were independently reviewed and confirmed by two professors specializing in educational measurement and evaluation prior to the scoring process. Consequently, the SBA item functioned as a criterion-referenced task with a clearly defined answer framework, allowing both human and automated raters to evaluate student responses against the same predetermined criteria.

“A teacher aims to enhance the interest of male students in reading books. To understand the factors influencing their reading interest, the teacher identifies the following variables:

Based on this scenario, respond to the following:

This question required the identification and classification of research variables. It had clear, universally accepted correct answers based on standard research methodology, with little room for ambiguity. Students were expected to correctly classify the variables as quantitative or qualitative, continuous or discrete, and identify control and confounding variables, all of which had specific, objective answers. This made the question ideal for objective scoring, where there was one correct set of responses based on established research principles. To determine whether the question in the study measured the intended traits, feedback was gathered from two professors specializing in educational measurement and evaluation.

### 2.5 Raters and scoring

#### 2.5.1 Human raters

Two experienced academicians who taught the Measurement and Evaluation course at the tertiary level served as human raters. Both were familiar with scoring different item types using rubrics and had a strong understanding of IRR and IRA. To minimize rater effects, the raters participated in training sessions that included detailed rubric review and discussion of consistent application (Reddy & Andrade, [2009](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR39 "Reddy, Y. M., & Andrade, H. (2009). A review of rubric use in higher education. Assessment.")
), followed by calibration exercises in which they independently scored the same sample responses to establish a shared scoring framework (Wendler et al., [2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR50 "Wendler, C., Glazer, N., & Cline, F. (2019). Examining the calibration process for raters of the GRE® General Test. ETS Research Report Series, 1-19. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1002/ets2.12245
                  
                ")
). These procedures were intended to support consistent rubric use and reduce subjective interpretation. Raters were given two days to score each question type. Scoring the 46 OE responses required an average of 234.6 min, whereas scoring the 69 SBA responses required 96.6 min.

#### 2.5.2 AI scoring and training

This study employed ChatGPT-4 and Gemini as automated raters rather than parameter-level fine-tuned models. Consistent with Bansal et al. ([2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR2 "Bansal, C., Pandey, K. K., Goel, R., Sharma, A., & Jangirala, S. (2023). Artificial intelligence (Ai) bias impacts: Classification framework for effective mitigation. Issues in Information Systems, 24(4), 367–389. 
                  https://doi.org/10.48009/4_iis_2023_128
                  
                ")
), who highlight the importance of targeted training for improving AI task performance, model behavior was shaped exclusively through structured, rubric-aligned prompting designed to parallel standard human rater calibration practices. No model weights or internal parameters were modified at any stage.

Separate, task-specific prompting and training protocols were developed for both item types. In line with few-shot learning principles (Duan et al., [2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR6 "Duan, R., Li, D., Tong, Q., Yang, T., Liu, X., & Liu, X. (2021). A survey of few-shot learning: An effective method for intrusion detection, Security and Communication Networks. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1155/2021/4259629
                  
                ")
), both models were trained using a limited set of annotated exemplar responses aligned with the scoring rubrics.

For the OE task, each model was instructed to score student responses using a 0–5 analytic rubric aligned with Bloom’s Taxonomy. The prompting protocol emphasized differentiation between measurement and evaluation, assessment of the feasibility and realism of proposed interventions, and evaluation of response clarity, depth, and conceptual coherence. Six annotated exemplar responses—one representing each score band from 0 to 5—were provided. Each exemplar included (a) the student response, (b) the assigned rubric score, and (c) a brief rationale explicitly linking salient response features to the rubric criteria. These exemplars were selected to illustrate varying degrees of conceptual understanding, practical reasoning, and evaluative depth.

For the SBA task, a comparable structured prompting approach was applied. Models were guided through key conceptual definitions, scoring rules, and four worked examples illustrating correct and incorrect identification and classification of variables, including dependent and independent variables, variable types, and control and confounding variables. Prompts explicitly instructed the models to apply rule-based scoring aligned with the analytic rubric and to penalize partially correct or incomplete classifications to ensure consistent application of criteria.

Across both task types, the AI models were required to generate not only a numerical score but also a brief justification explicitly referencing relevant rubric criteria. During pilot testing, when rubric-inconsistent scoring was observed, corrective feedback was provided by restating the appropriate rubric descriptor and supplying an additional aligned example. This feedback was limited strictly to prompt-level clarification and did not involve iterative rescoring of student responses or adaptive learning beyond the initial prompting framework.

During the scoring phase, student responses were uploaded individually to each AI platform. To mitigate prompt sensitivity and establish consistency, all scoring was conducted using a fixed prompt template for each task type, with no changes to wording, ordering, or exemplars across responses. Each student response was scored independently in a single pass, and models were not exposed to previously scored responses.

AI scoring was intentionally implemented as a static process to allow a controlled comparison of scoring outcomes under fixed conditions. Once prompted and familiarized with the rubric, the automated raters applied the scoring criteria across all responses in a single rating round. No iterative recalibration, adaptive modification, or examination of variability across multiple AI rating rounds was conducted. ### 2.6 Data analysis

The study involved four raters: ChatGPT 4, Gemini, and two human raters (Rater 1 and Rater 2). OE and SBA questions were rated on a 0–5 scale. Robinson’s A and Gwet’s AC2 were used to evaluate overall and pairwise IRA. For assessing IRR, Spearman’s Rho and the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) were applied. The coefficients employed in this study reflect different methodological perspectives. The ICC is derived through analysis of variance (Liljequist et al., [2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR29 "Liljequist, D., Elfving, B., & Roaldsen, S. (2019). K. Intraclass correlation-A discussion and demonstration of basic features. PloS one, 14(7), e0219854. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0219854
                  
                ")
), while Spearman’s rho is calculated based on rank correlation (Siegel, [1956](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR41 "Siegel, S. (1956). Nonparametric statistics for the behavioral sciences. Verlag nicht ermittelbar.")
). Both Robinson’s A and Gwet’s AC2 were used to assess IRA. Robinson’s A is noted for its simplicity and ease of interpretation, while Gwet’s AC2 was used for its superior stability compared to Cohen’s Kappa, a metric frequently used in research (Wongpakaran et al., [2013](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR53 "Wongpakaran, N., Wongpakaran, T., Wedding, D., & Gwet, K. L. (2013). A comparison of Cohen’s Kappa and Gwet’s AC1 when calculating inter-rater reliability coefficients: A study conducted with personality disorder samples. BMC Medical Research Methodology, 13(1), 61. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1186/1471-2288-13-61
                  
                ")
). R programming language packages were utilized, with the _irr_ package (Gamer et al., [2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR11 "Gamer, M., Lemon, J., & Singh, I. F. P. (2019). irr: Various coefficients of interrater reliability.")
) for Robinson’s A and ICC, and the _irrCAC_ package (Gwet, [2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR16 "Gwet, K. L. (2022). irrCAC: Computing chance-corrected agreement coefficients (CAC) (Version 1.0) [Computer software]. https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=irrCAC")
) for Gwet’s AC2.

For the OE item, skewness coefficients ranged from 0.12 to 0.61, while kurtosis coefficients ranged from − 1.20 to 0.57. For the SBA item, skewness coefficients ranged from − 0.18 to 0.46, and kurtosis coefficients ranged from − 1.25 to − 0.65. Because the skewness and kurtosis coefficients deviated from zero and the rating scale had an ordinal structure (0–5), Spearman’s rho correlation coefficient was calculated. Pearson correlation assumes that variables are continuous and satisfy pairwise normality (Leech et al., [2005](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR27 "Leech, N. L., Barrett, K. C., & Morgan, G. A. (2005). SPSS for intermediate statistics, use and interpretation. Lawrence Erlbaum Associates.")
); therefore, Spearman’s rho was considered a more appropriate option for the present dataset. Spearman’s rho was computed using the cor() function in R with the “spearman” method.

4 Discussion
------------

The present study investigated the IRR and IRA across two human raters and two AI models, with a focus on assessments in Turkish, a language often underrepresented in AI research. Both human and automated raters evaluated the same set of novice student responses using identical scoring rubrics; differences in agreement and reliability should therefore be interpreted as arising from rater mechanisms rather than from differences in student proficiency.

The study aimed to explore differences in scoring across two distinct question types: OE and SBA. Our findings shed light on how raters from different backgrounds differ in their scoring behaviors and how task type influences their assessment outcomes. In this study, human and automated raters were treated as methodologically distinct evaluative systems, each operating under different learning, calibration, and scoring dynamics; comparisons are therefore intended to illuminate task–rater interactions rather than to establish equivalence between human judgment and algorithmic evaluation. Although this study demonstrates differences in scoring reliability and agreement between human and automated raters, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence of score validity. High levels of agreement indicate consistency in rubric application but do not guarantee that scoring judgments accurately capture the quality, depth, or educational value of student responses. In particular, consistent scoring may coexist with construct underrepresentation or construct-irrelevant variance if the scoring criteria fail to reflect the full scope of the intended learning outcomes. Therefore, the present findings are best understood as evidence about rater behavior under shared scoring conditions, rather than as validation of the assessment instruments or the resulting score interpretations.

The findings indicate that automated models demonstrated higher IRR and IRA than human raters when scoring the OE question, whereas human raters performed slightly better on the SBA question. This contrast highlights the importance of task type and task structure in shaping scoring outcomes and offers a coherent framework for interpreting the mixed results reported in prior comparisons of AI and human assessment (e.g., Bui & Barrot, [2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR4 "Bui, N. M., & Barrot, J. S. (2024). ChatGPT as an automated essay scoring tool in the writing classrooms: how it compares with human scoring. Education and Information Technologies. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1007/s10639-024-12891-w
                  
                ")
; Li et al., [2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR28 "Li, J., Jangamreddy, N. K., Hisamoto, R., ., Bhansali, R., Dyda, A., ., Zaphir, L., & Glencross, M. (2024). AI-assisted marking: Functionality and limitations of ChatGPT in written assessment evaluation. Australasian Journal of Educational Technology, 40(4), 56–72. 
                  https://doi.org/10.14742/ajet.9463
                  
                ")
; Mizumoto & Eguchi, [2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR32 "Mizumoto, A., & Eguchi, M. (2023). Exploring the potential of using an AI language model for automated essay scoring. Research Methods in Applied Linguistics, 2(2), 1–13. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1016/j.rmal.2023.100050
                  
                ")
; Teckwani et al., [2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR45 "Teckwani, S. H., Wong, A. H. W., Luke, V., N., & Low, I. (2024). Accuracy and reliability of large language models in assessing learning outcomes achievement across cognitive domains. Advances in Physiology Education, 48, 904–914. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1152/advan.00137.2024
                  
                ")
). Rather than indicating an inherent advantage of one rater type over another, the results suggest that differences in agreement and reliability emerge from the interaction between rater type and the structural characteristics of assessment tasks.

From this perspective, the OE and SBA items differed substantially in their design properties. OE questions typically allow a wide range of acceptable responses and require evaluators to integrate multiple dimensions of performance, such as conceptual understanding, application, and critical evaluation (Lee et al., [2012](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR26 "Lee, Y., Kinzie, M. B., & Whittaker, J. V. (2012). Impact of online support for teachers’ open-ended questioning in preK science activities. Teaching and Teacher Education, 28, 568–577. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tate.2012.01.002
                  
                ")
). In contrast, SBA questions are more constrained, with clearly defined correct answers and rule-based scoring criteria (Kaya Uyanık & Ertuna, [2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR21 "Kaya Uyanik, G., & Ertuna, L. (2022). Examination of testlet effect in open-ended items. Sage Open, 12(1). 
                  https://doi.org/10.1177/21582440221079849
                  
                ")
). These structural differences are central to understanding why automated and human raters exhibited different patterns of consistency across the two task types. AI models, which apply scoring criteria in a uniform and repeatable manner, may be particularly well suited to handling the variability inherent in OE responses. Conversely, the constrained nature of SBA tasks appears to align more closely with human raters’ capacity for rapid and accurate rule-based judgments.

Although these patterns are consistent with predictions derived from Cognitive Load Theory, it is important to emphasize that cognitive load was not directly measured or manipulated in the present study. Instead, task complexity was inferred from observable task characteristics, such as response openness, degree of structure, number of evaluative dimensions, and rubric type. From a Cognitive Load Theory perspective (Sweller, [1988](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR42 "Sweller, J. (1988). Cognitive load during problem solving: Effects on learning. Cognitive Science, 12(2), 257–285. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog1202_4
                  
                ")
), OE tasks can be characterized as having higher intrinsic complexity, as they require the integration of multiple interacting elements, whereas SBA tasks involve lower intrinsic complexity due to their well-structured and rule-based nature. The observed differences in IRR and IRA are therefore interpreted as task-dependent patterns consistent with theoretical expectations, rather than as direct evidence that cognitive load caused the observed effects.

Dual Process Theory (Evans & Stanovich, [2013](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR9 "Evans, J. S., & Stanovich, K. E. (2013). Dual-process theories of higher cognition: Advancing the debate. Perspectives on Psychological Science, 8, 223–241. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691612460685
                  
                ")
) provides an additional interpretive lens for understanding these task-dependent patterns. Tasks with higher structural complexity may necessitate more deliberate, analytical processing, whereas simpler, well-defined tasks allow for faster and more consistent judgments. However, the present findings do not permit conclusions about the cognitive processes actually engaged by raters; rather, they suggest that task structure influences the conditions under which consistency in scoring is more or less likely to emerge for different rater types. Importantly, AI models are not subject to fluctuations in attentional resources or fatigue, which may contribute to their more stable performance across structurally complex tasks.

For the SBA item, human raters demonstrated slightly higher agreement and reliability than the AI models. This finding is consistent with the highly structured nature of the task, which minimizes ambiguity and reduces the need for integrative judgment. Under such conditions, human raters may apply scoring rules efficiently and consistently, with less opportunity for bias or interpretive divergence (Tversky & Kahneman, [1974](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR48 "Tversky, A., & Kahneman, D. (1974). Judgment under uncertainty: Heuristics and biases. Science, 185(4157), 1124–1131. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1126/science.185.4157.1124
                  
                ")
). Once again, this pattern should be understood as reflecting the alignment between task structure and rater characteristics, rather than as evidence of superior human performance per se.

The time required to score each item type further supports this task-based interpretation. Scoring OE responses required substantially more time from human raters than scoring the SBA responses, reflecting the greater complexity and evaluative demands of the OE task. While extended scoring time may increase the likelihood of cognitive strain (Gavelin et al., [2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR12 "Gavelin, H. M., Neely, S., Aronsson, A., Josefsson, I., M., & Andersson, L. (2023). Mental fatigue, cognitive performance and autonomic response following sustained mental activity in clinical burnout. Biological Psychology, 183. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biopsycho.2023.108661
                  
                ")
), the present study does not directly assess this possibility. Automated raters, by contrast, processed responses rapidly and consistently, which may partly explain their higher agreement and reliability on the OE task (Xiao et al., [2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR54 "Xiao, C., Ma, W., Song, Q., Xu, S. X., Zhang, K., Wang, Y., & Fu, Q. (2024). Human-AI collaborative essay scoring: A dual-process framework with LLMs. arXiv Preprint.ttps://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2401.06431")
). The SBA task, requiring less time and less integrative judgment, likely facilitated more efficient and consistent scoring by human raters. Taken together, these findings underscore the importance of considering task design and scoring demands when evaluating the role of automated and human raters in educational assessment and point toward the potential value of hybrid assessment approaches that capitalize on the complementary strengths of each.

Beyond differences in scoring consistency and efficiency, it is also important to consider whether human and automated raters assign comparable score levels. Significant differences emerged when comparing mean scores assigned by human and automated raters. For the OE item, GPT assigned the highest average score, followed by Gemini, with Rater 2 giving the lowest, indicating AI models scored more generously. These differences, statistically significant, suggest that while AI models provided consistent scoring, their tendencies diverged from human raters, particularly for the OE item. For the SBA item, mean scores were more similar across raters but still showed significant differences, with GPT assigning the highest and Gemini the lowest scores. These findings highlight that even for objective tasks, AI scoring may differ slightly from human raters, emphasizing the need to align AI scoring systems with human judgment in standardized testing.

The variation in performance between AI models and human raters across different question types may be attributed to limitations in the few-shot learning approach used to train the AI models. Training on a limited set of examples can lead to consistent but systematically flawed application of scoring criteria, potentially introducing unintended biases (Rinta-Kahila et al., [2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-026-13949-7#ref-CR40 "Rinta-Kahila, T., Someh, I., Gillespie, N., Indulska, M., & Gregor, S. (2021). Algorithmic decision-making and system destructiveness: A case of automatic debt recovery. European Journal of Information Systems, 31(3), 313–338. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1080/0960085X.2021.1960905
                  
                ")
). The stronger performance of AI models on OE responses suggests that the training data effectively captured patterns relevant for evaluating complex answers. However, the higher average scores assigned by AI, particularly GPT, indicate possible overgeneralization in their scoring tendencies, such as consistently assigning higher scores than human raters. Conversely, the slightly weaker performance of AI on the SBA item underscores potential gaps in the training process that may have limited the models’ ability to fully align with human judgment. It is recommended that the effectiveness of alternative training models be explored to assess whether they can provide improved alignment and reliability in AI scoring within the context of educational assessments.

The findings suggest that automated scoring systems may be most effectively deployed within hybrid assessment models rather than as standalone replacements for human judgment. For OE responses, automated scoring systems demonstrated relatively high consistency and may be well suited for roles such as initial scoring, rubric-based triage, or flagging responses for subsequent human moderation. Such approaches have the potential to reduce rater workload while preserving human oversight for borderline cases or responses requiring pedagogical sensitivity.

For highly structured tasks such as SBA items, human raters exhibited slightly higher agreement and reliability, indicating that traditional human scoring remains both efficient and effective in these contexts. Assessment designers may therefore consider aligning rater type with task structure, leveraging automated scoring for cognitively complex, high-variance responses while reserving human judgment for tasks that benefit from contextual interpretation or instructional insight.

More broadly, these results underscore the importance of assessment design in shaping the effectiveness of AI integration. Rubric clarity, task structure, and transparency of scoring criteria appear to be at least as critical as the choice of rater itself in determining whether automated scoring enhances or undermines assessment quality.

5 Conclusion
------------

This study provides valuable insights into the IRR and IRA between human raters and two automated raters. The findings highlight the complex relationship between task type, task-related cognitive demands, and scoring behaviors, with automated raters demonstrating greater consistency in evaluating the OE question, while human raters showed closer alignment with AI on more structured SBA task. These results emphasize the need to consider task-specific demands when integrating AI into educational assessment. Despite these contributions, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study involved a relatively small and homogeneous sample of third-year undergraduate students drawn from a single department and course. This narrow educational context limits statistical generalizability, as patterns of rater agreement and reliability may differ across disciplines, educational levels, or institutional settings. The findings should therefore be interpreted as context-specific and indicative rather than definitive.

Second, the study employed a single OE item and a single SBA item. While this design does not capture the full range of assessment formats used in educational evaluation—such as extended essays, case analyses, or problem-solving tasks—it allowed for a focused comparison of two contrasting task structures under controlled conditions. Future studies should incorporate multiple items per format and a broader range of assessment types to examine the stability of these findings.

While cognitive load theory informed interpretation, cognitive load itself was not directly assessed. Future research could strengthen causal inference by integrating direct cognitive-load measures (e.g., subjective rating scales or physiological indicators) or by experimentally manipulating task complexity.

Although the automated raters demonstrated high reliability and agreement, the present study did not systematically examine potential sources of bias in AI scoring. Factors such as sensitivity to language style, cultural references, narrative creativity, or unconventional response structures may influence automated evaluations and could have implications for fairness and validity. While the structured prompting and rubric-based training were intended to mitigate subjective bias, future research should explicitly investigate differential AI scoring across diverse linguistic, cultural, and expressive contexts to better assess equity in AI-assisted assessment.

This study examined only two large language models—ChatGPT-4 and Gemini—which, although widely used and representative of current state-of-the-art AI systems, do not capture the full diversity of available AI architectures. Other models may differ in their sensitivity to prompt design, response structure, or scoring criteria. Consequently, the findings should not be generalized to AI scoring systems broadly, and future research should compare a wider range of models to examine the robustness and generalizability of AI-based scoring outcomes.

In addition to limitations related to model selection, the language of student responses introduces another important dimension that may shape automated rater performance. Language may moderate automated scoring performance through multiple mechanisms, including differences in training data volume, syntactic complexity, and culturally embedded response patterns. Turkish, as a morphologically rich language with flexible word order, may pose distinct challenges for automated scoring systems compared to English. Future multilingual studies should explicitly examine whether observed differences arise from linguistic structure, cultural framing of educational responses, or uneven representation in model training corpora.
