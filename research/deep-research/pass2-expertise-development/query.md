Research question: What distinguishes intermediate, advanced and expert guitarists and improvisers, and how does musical expertise develop over years of practice in adults?

Specifically investigate:
1. Skill differences between intermediate, advanced and expert guitarists/improvisers across hearing, technique, rhythm/timing, harmony, expression, repertoire knowledge and interaction (expert-novice comparison studies, Solstad's work on expertise in jazz guitar improvisation, Wopereis et al. on development of improvisational expertise in jazz, Norgaard's studies of improvising musicians).
2. Long-term developmental trajectories and stages: what is learned first, which skills appear to be prerequisites, and how skills co-develop.
3. The deliberate practice debate: Ericsson's deliberate practice framework versus the Macnamara, Hambrick & Oswald meta-analyses (2014, 2016) and later re-analyses; what counts as deliberate practice, retrospective hour estimates, and the role of other factors (starting age, aptitude, personality, instruction quality). Note that explained variance is not the share of an individual's skill caused by practice.
4. Plateaus in adult skill learning: causes (automaticity / "OK plateau", practice quality, motivation), evidence on how plateaus are overcome, and adult-specific learning considerations (age-related constraints, prior experience).
5. Sustainability over years: practice adherence and motivation in adult amateur musicians; playing-related musculoskeletal disorders and overuse injuries in guitarists, practice volume and rest, and evidence for prevention.
6. Counterevidence: findings that practice amount poorly predicts expertise, or that expert-novice differences do not map onto teachable skills.

Research context (applies to the whole brief):
- Learners: adult intermediate guitarists who have plateaued. They know basic chords and scales and can improvise over familiar backing tracks, but progress has stalled. They commit roughly 2 hours per day for 2 years (~1,400 hours), which may include listening, reflection and breaks.
- Goal: advanced musicianship (hearing, technique, rhythm, harmony, expression, repertoire, improvisation, playing with others). Do not assume or promise professional or world-class attainment from an hour count.
- Purpose: decisions for an AI-supported, adaptive guitar and musicianship tutor.
- Source preferences: peer-reviewed empirical studies, meta-analyses and systematic reviews first; then expert or practitioner primary sources (books, dissertations, conference papers by researchers or recognized educators). Avoid YouTube videos, SEO blogs, app marketing pages and forums where possible. Favor queries likely to surface journals (e.g. Journal of Research in Music Education, Psychology of Music, Musicae Scientiae, Frontiers in Psychology, Music Education Research, ISMIR proceedings), PubMed Central, ERIC, arXiv, university repositories.
- Actively seek counterevidence, null results and failed replications, not only supporting findings.
- For every finding, note population (age, level, instrument, genre), sample size, study design and duration, and whether the result was measured at delayed retention or transfer to unfamiliar material. Flag when evidence comes from other instruments, children, or non-music motor learning and may not generalize to adult guitarists.
