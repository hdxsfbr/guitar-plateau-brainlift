24Abstract
25Background:  Playing-related  disorders  (PRP)  are  among  the  most  common 
26occupational  health  problems  affecting  musicians;  however,  no  prior  meta-
27analysis  has  focused  exclusively  on  guitarists.  Existing  reviews  often  group 
28plucked-string  players  with  bowed-string  or  other  instrumentalists,  overlooking 
29the unique biomechanical demands of guitar performance. In addition, the widely 
30used term PRMD (playing-related musculoskeletal disorders) frequently conflates 
31clinically  diagnosed  conditions  with  self-reported  symptoms,  limiting  clinical 
32clarity.  We  propose  a  classification  under  the  umbrella  term  playing-related 
33problems    (PRP)    comprising:    (1)    PRMS—self-reported    playing-related 
34musculoskeletal  symptoms;  (2)  PRMD—clinically  diagnosed  playing-related 
35musculoskeletal  disorders;  and  (3)  PRND—clinically  diagnosed  playing-related 
36neurological  disorders.  All  are  secondary  to  instrument  playing  and  defined  by 
37the presence of a symptom (PRMS) or disorder (PRMD or PRND) that interferes 
38with  the  musician’s  ability  to  perform  at  their  usual  level. Methods:  Following 
39PRISMA  guidelines,  we  conducted  a  systematic  review  and  meta-analysis  of 
40PRP prevalence in guitarists. Studies were classified by PRP subtype. Random-
41effects  meta-analyses  of  proportions  were  performed,  with  subgroup  analyses 
42and mixed-effects meta-regressions to explore heterogeneity. Funnel plots and 
43Egger’s test were used to assess potential publication bias. Results: Twenty-two 
44cross-sectional studies were included (n = 2,119). Pooled prevalence was 51.8% 
45for  any  PRP,  64.5%  for  PRMS,  42.2%  for  PRMD,  and  14.5%  for  PRND.  In 
46analyses  restricted  to  musculoskeletal  outcomes  (PRMS  +  PRMD),  specifying 
47guitar   type   substantially   reduced   heterogeneity,   with   classical,   bass,   and 
48flamenco guitarists showing the highest prevalences. Meta-regression identified

61Introduction
62Playing-related  musculoskeletal  disorders  (PRMD)  are  among  the  most 
63common  occupational  health  problems  affecting  both  professional  and  student 
64musicians,  with  a  reported  lifetime  prevalence  ranging  from  39%  to  87%, 
65depending  on  the  population  and  definition  used  [1,2].  These  conditions  can 
66severely limit practice time, diminish performance quality, and ultimately shorten 
67professional  careers  [3].  Within  this  context,  guitarists  represent  a  particularly 
68vulnerable population due to the unique and strenuous biomechanical demands 
69of  their  practice.  The  traditional  seated  posture  of  classical  guitar,  with  the 
70instrument  resting  on  a  raised  thigh,  creates  asymmetric  spinal  loading  and 
71prolonged  trunk  flexion,  which  are  strongly  associated  with  chronic  back  and 
72shoulder pain. The demands on the hands are equally extreme and distinct: the 
73fretting hand must apply continuous finger pressure and maintain awkward wrist

75for several hours a day [4].
76Although evidence points to a high prevalence of these issues in guitarists 
77[5–7], a consolidated understanding is hindered by several key methodological 
78limitations.  One  major  challenge  is  the  inconsistent  and  imprecise  use  of 
79terminology.  The  term  PRMD  is  often  applied  broadly,  conflating  clinically 
80diagnosed  musculoskeletal  conditions  such  as  tendinitis  with  any  self-reported 
81symptom  of  pain  or  discomfort  that  interferes,  to  some  degree,  with  musical 
82performance  [7–9].  As  noted  by  previous  authors,  this  conceptual  ambiguity 
83complicates both research synthesis and clinical interpretation [10]. Furthermore, 
84it  is  compounded  by  a  lack  of  specificity  regarding  guitarist  subpopulations,  as 
85most  studies  fail  to  differentiate  between  playing  different  type  of  guitars  (e.g., 
86classical,  electric,  acoustic)  or  aggregate  them  into  a  single  group,  introducing 
87substantial heterogeneity and limiting the conclusions [11]. 
88To  address  these  issues,  we  propose  the  broader  construct  Playing-
89Related  Problems  (PRP),  defined  as  any  playing-associated  condition  that 
90prevents  the  guitarist  from  performing  at  their  previous  level.  Within  this 
91framework,    we    distinguish    three    subcategories:    (1)    Playing-Related 
92Musculoskeletal Symptoms (PRMS) — self-reported musculoskeletal complaints 
93perceived  by  the  musician  as  limiting  or  impairing  their  ability  to  perform  the 
94instrument  as  they  were  accustomed  to,  without  a  confirmed  diagnosis;  (2) 
95Playing-Related Musculoskeletal Disorders (PRMD) — musculoskeletal injuries, 
96disorders,  or  diseases  diagnosed  by  a  healthcare  professional  (e.g.,  overuse 
97syndrome, tendinitis); and (3) Playing-Related Neurological Disorders (PRND) —

100This   conceptual   clarification   is   central   to   our   analytical   approach. 
101Accordingly,  we  conducted  a  comprehensive  systematic  review  and  meta-

103relevant    prevalence    estimates    by    explicitly    accounting    for    definitional 
104heterogeneity,  stratifying  data  by  problem  type,  and  comparing  prevalence 
105between  studies  of  classical  guitarists  and  those  with  mixed  or  unspecified 
106guitarist populations.

109Protocol and Registration
110This  systematic  review  and  meta-analysis  were  conducted  and  reported 
111following  the  PRISMA  (Preferred  Reporting  Items  for  Systematic  Reviews  and 
112Meta-Analyses)  guidelines  [12].  Details  of  compliance  are  provided  in  S1  File. 
113The   study   protocol   was   pre-registered   in   the   PROSPERO   international 
114prospective register of systematic reviews (registration no. CRD420251069560). 
115It  should  be  noted  that  while  the  initial  protocol  title  and  objectives  focused 
116exclusively on classical guitarists, the systematic literature search revealed that 
117most of the available studies did not specify the guitarist type. The review&#39;s scope 
118was broadened to include all guitar types to address this limitation in the evidence 
119and  provide  a  more  comprehensive  analysis.  Consequently,  the  analysis  plan

124Eligibility Criteria and PICO Framework
125Population was defined as guitarists of any age, with an emphasis, but not 
126exclusivity,  on  classical  guitarists  (formally  trained  in  classical  technique).  We 
127also included studies of a mixed population of guitarists, provided data specific to 
128classical or acoustic guitarists that were available, and regular guitar playing (≥3 
129months of formal or self-directed practice). In risk-factor analyses, the “exposure” 
130of interest included high daily practice time, posture variables (e.g., seated with 
131a footstool vs. alternative support or standing posture), psychosocial stress, and 
132a  history  of  prior  injury.  In  addition  to  the  prevalence  estimations  in  the  meta-
133analysis,   we   compared   classical   versus   (1)   non-classical   guitarists   (e.g., 
134acoustic, bass, electric, flamenco) and (2) within neurological vs musculoskeletal 
135problems.  The  primary  outcome  was  studies  that  reported  any  PRP,  which 
136included  PRMD,  defined  as  conditions  requiring  health  professional  evaluation 
137for  confirmation;  2)  self-reported  musculoskeletal  symptoms,  identified  solely 
138through  questionnaires  or  surveys  with  some  grade  affecting  of  the  guitar 
139technique  (PRMS);  and  PRND,  such  as  clinical  diagnosed  by  an  health  expert 
140(such  a  neuropathies  or  focal  dystonia).  We  included  observational  studies 
141(cross-sectional, cohort, and case-control) and clinical studies (e.g., case series) 
142that provided data on prevalence or risk factors. Intervention studies (e.g., trials 
143of   preventive   exercises)   were   also   considered   if   they   reported   baseline 
144prevalence or other relevant observational data, although none were ultimately 
145found that met our criteria. We excluded studies where guitarists were grouped

150Information sources and search strategy
151We  conducted  a  systematic  literature  search  across  five  electronic 
152databases, PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, Dimensions, and PsycINFO. The 
153search  strategy  combined  terms  related  to  musicians  and  musculoskeletal 
154disorders,  for  example:  “(guitar  OR  guitarist*)  AND  (musculoskeletal  OR  injury 
155OR pain OR disorder)”. No date and language restrictions were applied, and the 
156final searches were conducted on June 7, 2025. In addition to database searches, 
157we  performed  snowball  sampling  of  references  using  Connected  Papers  and 
158citation  tracking.  We  also  manually  searched  the  journal  Medical  Problems  of 
159Performing  Artists  (a  core  journal  in  performing  arts  medicine)  and  reviewed 
160conference  proceedings  in  performing  arts  health  for  relevant  abstracts,  and 
161searches  were  not  limited  to  language  restrictions.  The  entire  strategy  for  all 
162databases is provided in the S2 File.

180Study Risk of Bias Assessment
181We assessed the risk of bias in the included studies using criteria adapted 
182from  the  Joanna  Briggs  Institute  checklist  for  prevalence  purposes  [13].  Key 
183factors  considered  included  the  sampling  method  (random  or  convenience 
184sample), response rate, case definition of musculoskeletal disorder (and whether 
185it  required  symptoms  to  affect  play),  recall  period  for  prevalence  (to  assess 
186potential recall bias), and statistical rigor in any risk factor analysis. Overall, most 
187studies were cross-sectional in design, which inherently limits the ability to make 
188causal inferences.

421Quality of the evidence
422All included studies were cross‑sectional and most had moderate to severe 
423methodological limitations. Common issues included non‑probabilistic sampling, 
424self‑selection of participants, inconsistent or vague case definitions, and reliance 
425on self‑report without clinical confirmation. Many studies did not report guitar type, 
426playing level, or training background. According to GRADE criteria, the certainty 
427of the evidence for all prevalence estimates was judged to be low, due to risk of 
428bias, indirectness, and inconsistency.

436guitarists.  Previous  systematic  reviews  have  offered  valuable  perspectives  on 
437musician health, consistently confirming the high prevalence of PRMDs and the 
438methodological heterogeneity within this research field. For example, the work of 
439Fu  and  Loo  identified  that  string  and  keyboard  instrument  students  had  the 
440highest  probabilities  of  PRMD  [32],  while  Rotter  et  al.  lamented  the  lack  of

442[10]. A systematic mapping review further called explicitly for instrument-specific 
443subgroup analyses and highlighted substantial evidence gaps across instrument 
444families [33]. However, a critical and recurrent limitation in these reviews is the 
445tendency  to  group  instruments  with  fundamentally  different  biomechanical 
446demands. This limitation is particularly evident in the classification of guitarists. 
447Notably, no previous systematic review has focused its analysis exclusively on 
448this population. Influential reviews such as Kochem and Silva, which addressed 
449“string    players,”    focused    their    analysis    exclusively    on    bowed    string 
450instrumentalists  (violins,  violas,  cellos,  and  double  basses),  omitting  guitarists 
451and other plucked-string players [11]. Other works, such as Rodríguez-Gude et 
452al.,  identified  individual  studies  on  guitarists  but  presented  main  conclusions 
453grouped by instrumental families (e.g., upper vs lower strings) and underscored 
454the small number of articles per instrument as a limitation [1]. Grouping guitarists 
455with  violinists  under  a  generic  “string  instrument”  category  obscures  posture, 
456asymmetric loading, and hand-specific kinematics (fretting vs 
457plucking/strumming) that differ fundamentally from bowing.

523Conclusions
524This  systematic  review  and  meta-analysis  consolidate,  for  the  first  time, 
525the prevalence of PRPs in guitarists, demonstrating that these disorders affect a 
526substantial    proportion    of    musicians    and    can    significantly    compromise 
527performance  capacity.  By  disentangling  problem  subtypes  and  instrument 
528categories,  our  findings  reveal  that  biomechanical  demands  and  disorder 
529classification   are   critical   determinants   of   risk   and   heterogeneity.   While 
530methodological limitations in the primary literature require cautious interpretation, 
531the   consistent   patterns   observed—together   with   suggestive   evidence   on 
532modifiable risk factors such as warm-ups, breaks, and physical activity—offer a
