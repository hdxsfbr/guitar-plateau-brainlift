Executive summary

This report synthesizes empirical and practice‑based evidence about what separates intermediate, advanced and expert guitarists and improvisers; how musical expertise develops in adults over years of practice; causes and remedies for plateaus; the deliberate practice debate; long‑term sustainability including playing‑related injuries; and counterevidence that practice amount alone predicts expertise. The evidence base combines peer‑reviewed quantitative meta‑analyses, longitudinal and cross‑sectional studies, systematic reviews, and practice‑based qualitative work specific to jazz guitar. Where possible I note population, sample size, design and limitations and flag findings that come from other instruments, children, or non‑music motor learning.

Key takeaways (quick)

- Skill differences between intermediate, advanced and expert players show domain‑specific patterns: hearing (aural skills), pattern libraries (chunking/templates), timing/rhythm control, harmonic command, expressive control, repertoire depth and interactive fluency. Evidence comes from mixed methods: qualitative interviews with world‑class jazz guitarists (Solstad), experimental studies of musicians vs non‑musicians, and cross‑sectional comparisons in the literature.
- Development is nonlinear: rapid early gains, plateaus, and secondary accelerations (logarithmic trajectory). Some skills (basic technique, basic scale/chord vocabulary, beat keeping and listening) are prerequisites; advanced skills co‑develop via focused variation: chunking + schema building + contextual templates.
- Practice quality matters as much as quantity. Meta‑analyses produce different effect size estimates depending on measurement and correction: corrected correlations between task‑relevant practice and achievement can be large (Platz et al. r = 0.61 corrected), while other reanalyses estimate purposeful/deliberate practice explains ~30%–60% of variance depending on assumptions (Hambrick et al.; Ericsson & Harwell). Retrospective hour estimates and poor operationalization of practice types cause variance in conclusions.
- Plateaus are common and multi‑causal: automaticity (OK plateau), poor practice design (low challenge, low feedback), motivation, and constraints of adult learning. Overcoming them requires improved practice quality (deliberate/purposeful practice with feedback), distributed varied practice, challenge, and social/performative contexts.
- Long‑term sustainability: many adult guitarists sustain practice but musculoskeletal problems are common. A preprint meta‑analysis of guitarists reported pooled prevalences: any playing‑related problem 51.8%; PRMS 64.5%; clinically diagnosed PRMD 42.2%; PRND 14.5% (22 cross‑sectional studies, N=2,119). Evidence quality is heterogeneous; regular short rests (e.g., 5 minutes after ~25 minutes) are recommended based on interventional and review evidence.
- Counterevidence exists: some studies find practice amount poorly predicts expertise and skilled differences are not wholly teachable. Measurement error, omitted variables (aptitude, instruction), and domain differences matter.

1. What distinguishes intermediate, advanced and expert guitarists/improvisers? (domain by domain)

Methodology note: Evidence for each domain comes from mixed sources: qualitative practice‑based studies (Solstad’s dissertation/monograph), cross‑sectional comparisons in music psychology, and broader reviews/meta‑analyses. Where study details are available I list them; many practice‑based insights are from small‑N qualitative inquiries and should be treated as high‑resolution practitioner evidence rather than generalizable population statistics.

1.1 Hearing (aural skills)

- Differences: Experts show superior relative and absolute pitch tasks relevant to musical context (e.g., fast, accurate identification of chord qualities, scales, functional degrees), better ability to perceive and predict harmonic changes, and finer timbral discrimination in performance contexts. Musicians (adult) show enhanced executive function and working memory tied to auditory tasks (PLOS ONE study; sample details: adult musicians vs non‑musicians; neuroimaging evidence indicated greater pre‑SMA/SMA and right VLPFC activation during rule representation and task switching; exact N not listed in the provided learning but the study is peer‑reviewed).
- Evidence type: experimental comparisons and neuroimaging; generalization: many studies use classical or mixed instrument samples — direct guitar‑specific psychophysics is limited.
- Transfer/retention: Studies showing musician advantage usually measure immediate tasks; few measure transfer to unfamiliar material longitudinally.

1.2 Technique (mechanics of playing)

- Differences: Experts demonstrate economy of motion, anticipatory finger and wrist positioning, advanced left‑hand independence, controlled right‑hand articulation, and reliable tone production across dynamic ranges. Solstad’s interviews with world‑class jazz guitarists highlight the role of template libraries and physically adapted fingering solutions.
- Evidence: Much of this is practice‑based (Solstad’s five world‑class jazz guitarists: Lage Lund, Jack Wilkins, Ben Monder, Rez Abbasi, Adam Rogers). Quantitative ergonomic/technique literature for classical guitar is sparser; injury research and ergonomic tools (Rousseau 2021; Bonner 2024) indicate technique interacts with injury risk.
- Transfer/retention: Technical skills are motoric and show retention but require maintenance; transfer across styles is variable and depends on schema breadth.

1.3 Rhythm/timing

- Differences: Experts show tighter microtiming control, consistent internal pulse, flexible subdivision manipulation and superior polyrhythmic perception. Advanced players can maintain groove in variable contexts and adapt timing to ensemble cues; experts can purposefully micro‑displace or stretch timing for expression while maintaining ensemble stability.
- Evidence: Controlled studies on timing often use tapping tasks and sensorimotor paradigms; specific guitar studies are less common. Longitudinal monitoring of jazz improvisation shows timing stability evolves with practice (study in MH v25 noted logarithmic progression).

1.4 Harmony (functional understanding)

- Differences: Experts possess larger schema of harmonic templates and reharmonization strategies, immediate functional harmonic analysis, and fluency in voice‑leading and substitution. Intermediate players may rely on scale/chord shapes; advanced players develop voice‑leading and chord substitution fluency.
- Evidence: Solstad and Collantes identify chunking, schema and templates as core strategies; these are practice‑based, qualitative but grounded in cognitive theory. Empirical training studies vary; many cover theory instruction effects rather than improvisation transfer.

1.5 Expression / dynamics / phrasing

- Differences: Expert players control micro‑dynamics, articulation, vibrato, timbre shifts and phrasing shaped by longer time horizons (motivic-level phrasing). They use expressive timing strategically and shape lines structurally across form.
- Evidence: Practitioner interviews (Solstad) and experimental measures of musicality (e.g., video‑based practice intervention in adult beginner classical guitar; performance measures showed improvements on musicality, tone, rhythm and technical security after weekly video practice/assessment — sample details: thesis-based experimental study; p=.037 for group differences).

1.6 Repertoire knowledge

- Differences: Experts have broader and deeper repertoires, multiple arrangements/mappings for tunes, and quick recall for form/changes. Repertoire serves as a memory scaffold for improvisation (templates and chunk reuse).
- Evidence: Qualitative and cross‑sectional observations; not often quantified in controlled studies.

1.7 Interaction (playing with others)

- Differences: Experts show superior adaptive listening, predictive timing, conversational turn‑taking, comping sensitivity, and real‑time harmonic/melodic negotiation. Solstad and Collantes emphasize flexible listening and social/contextual adaptation as central to expertise.
- Evidence: Norgaard’s studies of improvising musicians (not fully detailed in provided learnings) and Solstad’s interplay analyses provide qualitative support. Experimental transfer studies of ensemble skills are rarer. Evidence often derived from jazz contexts; generalization to rock/blues/pop may require adaptation.

Population & design notes: Solstad’s monograph is practice‑based with five elite jazz guitarists (qualitative); general experimental evidence often uses mixed musicians/multi‑instrument samples with variable Ns. This means domain‑specific, generalizable effect sizes for guitarists are sparse.

2. Long‑term developmental trajectories and stages

2.1 Empirical pattern: logarithmic growth with plateaus

- A repeated finding (including longitudinal monitoring in jazz improvisation) is a logarithmic trajectory: rapid early gains, identifiable plateaus, and occasional secondary accelerations. The longitudinal study referenced (MH v25.82965) reported this pattern; sample and duration details were not specified in the learning but the shape mirrors motor and cognitive skill learning models.

2.2 Skills typically learned first (prerequisites)

- Early phase (first months to years):
  - Basic technique: fretting, basic right‑hand control, economy of motion.
  - Basic chord vocabulary and scale patterns (major, minor, pentatonic, dominant scales).
  - Pulse keeping and basic rhythmic subdivision.
  - Closed‑form ear skills: sing/echo short melodies, identify intervals and simple chord qualities.
  - Repertoire: a small set of tunes/voicings that become automatic.
  - These form prerequisites for later skills like flexible reharmonization, motif development, advanced comping.

- Why these are prerequisites: They provide motor economy, reduce cognitive load, and create a minimal template library for improvisation and interaction. Without basic technique and pulse, advanced expressive manipulations and rapid harmonic operations are fragile.

2.3 Co‑development and sequencing

- Chunking and template formation co‑develop with repertoire growth and listening: as players learn tunes and typical licks, they create larger chunks and context‑dependent schemas.
- Harmonic understanding deepens through repeated exposure to progressions in repertoire and through explicit functional analysis instruction.
- Rhythm and interaction skills improve with ensemble playing (real partners) and focused rhythm practice (metronome, polyrhythm tasks).
- Cognitive and executive functions (working memory, switching) also co‑develop with musical training but are confounded with preexisting differences (PLOS ONE evidence for musician advantages in executive function).

2.4 Timescales and expectations for the plateaued adult learner in the brief

- The learner described: adult, intermediate, ~1,400 hours over 2 years (approx. 2 hours/day). Empirically, this amount can produce large early gains and consolidation, but will often fall inside plateau phases unless practice quality increases.
- Recommendation: aim for targeted restructuring of practice to emphasize deliberate/purposeful activities (see section 3), varied challenge, incremental increases in task difficulty, and regular performance‑like testing to trigger transfer.

3. The deliberate practice debate (mechanisms, evidence, measurement)

3.1 Core definitional issues

- Ericsson’s framework: deliberate practice requires tasks specifically designed to improve performance, immediate feedback, error correction, full attention and repetition with gradual refinement; Ericsson (2021) reiterates five external criteria and distinguishes purposeful practice from mere accumulation when teacher guidance is absent.
- Macnamara/McNamara & Hambrick (2014; Macnamara et al.) and related meta‑analyses challenged the primacy of practice hours, arguing much variance is unaccounted for by practice alone and highlighting influences of innate factors and starting age. Hambrick et al. (2020) and others refined estimates of variance explained by practice when correcting for psychometric attenuation.

3.2 Quantitative summaries from recent meta‑analyses and reanalyses (from learnings)

- Platz et al. (2014): meta‑analysis of 13 studies (N = 788) reported an aggregated corrected correlation between task‑relevant practice and musical achievement r = 0.61 (95% CI 0.54–0.67).
- Hambrick et al. (2020): across six chess and eight music studies, average correlations between retrospective practice measures and performance were r = 0.49 (chess) and r = 0.43 (music); after psychometric correction the reliable variance explained by deliberate practice was 34% for chess and 29.9% for music.
- Ericsson & Harwell (2019): stricter criteria reanalysis found accumulated purposeful/deliberate practice explained substantially more variance — reporting 29% (raw) and 61% after attenuation correction — highlighting sensitivity to assumptions and measurement correction.

3.3 Measurement problems that drive disagreement

- Retrospective hour estimates are noisy: recall bias inflates or deflates reported hours. Effects on correlations are substantial.
- Poor operationalization of what constitutes practice: task‑relevant activity, deliberate practice, purposeful practice, and general musical experiences (listening, ensemble playing) are often lumped together.
- Different domains and achievement measures: sight‑reading vs improvisation vs competition ratings are not interchangeable.

3.4 Role of other factors

- Starting age: early starters gain advantages, especially in absolute pitch and early sensorimotor consolidation; many effects are stronger for some styles/instruments.
- Aptitude and personality: working memory, auditory imagery, and motivation/personality (conscientiousness) influence learning trajectories.
- Instruction quality: teacher feedback and well‑structured curricula amplify practice effectiveness; Ericsson emphasizes individualized feedback as essential for deliberate practice.

3.5 Practical conclusion for the adult plateaued learner

- Focus on practice quality: design sessions that meet deliberate/purposeful criteria where possible (clear goals, immediate feedback, repetition with incremental challenge). If individualized teacher input is unavailable, incorporate high‑quality automated feedback (audio/visual alignment, pitch/timing feedback, model performance comparisons) and structured practice protocols.
- Use objective practice logging (audio/automatic timestamps) rather than retrospective reports for research/diagnostics.

4. Plateaus in adult skill learning (causes and remediation)

4.1 Causes

- Automaticity / OK plateau: early automatic routines produce acceptable performance but limit further gains because practice becomes stereotyped and low‑challenge.
- Poor practice quality: lack of goal specificity, infrequent feedback, excessive mindless repetition.
- Lack of transfer tasks or challenge: too much practice on familiar material and too little on unfamiliar or performance‑like tasks.
- Motivation and affect: boredom, low intrinsic motivation, and lack of social reinforcement reduce engagement.
- Adult constraints: reduced neural plasticity relative to children for some functions, life‑role constraints (time, fatigue), and entrenched motor patterns that are harder to change.

4.2 Evidence on overcoming plateaus

- Active interventions: deliberate practice with expert feedback, interleaving of related tasks, variable practice that increases contextual interference (which aids transfer), and goal‑directed micro‑goals have empirical support across motor and cognitive domains (many experimental studies outside guitar; longitudinal monitoring in jazz showed secondary accelerations following interventions).
- Social/performance triggers: playing with others, public performance or recording sessions create pressure and novel contexts that spur transfer and overcome complacency (practice studies and teacher reports endorse this).
- Cognitive approaches: chunk recombination, explicit modeling of templates/schemata (Solstad/Collantes), and cognitive reframing of goals (process vs product) help bypass habitual patterns.

4.3 Adult‑specific considerations

- Emphasize distributed practice, explicit concept learning (functional harmony, templates), and technique re‑training with slow practice and motor imagery.
- Incorporate motivation maintenance (goal setting, periodization, social ensembles) and time‑efficient high‑quality practice methods.

5. Sustainability over years: adherence, injuries, rest

5.1 Practice adherence and motivation

- Adults commonly sustain practice for years but adherence fluctuates with life events. Interventions that improve self‑efficacy and provide external structure (video assessment, weekly feedback) increase practice quality and maintenance; the video practice study in adult beginner classical guitar showed performance improvements and raised self‑efficacy in the experimental group (thesis study; sample and method: weekly video-based practice/assessment; p = .037 observed group difference for musicality/technical measures).

5.2 Playing‑related musculoskeletal disorders (PRMD) and injury prevalence

- Systematic review/meta‑analysis evidence (preprint Castillo‑Barragán et al., 2025) pooled 22 cross‑sectional guitar studies (N = 2,119) and reported high prevalence estimates: any playing‑related problem 51.8%; PRMS 64.5%; clinically diagnosed PRMD 42.2%; PRND 14.5%. Note: this is a preprint and included cross‑sectional studies with heterogenous methodologies.
- Rotter et al. (2019) systematic review (109 articles across multiple designs) concluded the evidence is heterogeneous and of limited quality; causality that being a musician causes musculoskeletal disorder remains low.
- For classical guitar, specific claims about posture supports are not supported by high‑quality evidence; no conclusive evidence that alternative supports reduce PRMD risk (systematic review of 13 papers).

5.3 Prevention and best practices

- Breaks: Chan & Ackermann (Frontiers in Psychology, 2014) recommend regular short rest breaks; evidence suggests breaks are protective — practical guideline: 5‑minute break after every ~25 minutes of playing (Pomodoro‑style schedule favored in some studies).
- Ergonomics and assessment tools: Rousseau (2021) developed an Injury Risk Factors Questionnaire and a Postural Analysis Tool that can be integrated into screening/monitoring.
- Cross‑training, strength and mobility exercises, warm‑ups and cool‑downs, and technique adjustments reduce risk in practice; recent applied device research (Bonner 2024) tested a guitar support device for classical guitarists.

6. Counterevidence: practice amount sometimes poorly predictive

- Multiple high‑quality meta‑analyses and reanalyses show mixed results: practice correlates with achievement but does not account for all variance; estimates of variance explained range widely depending on measurement correction and inclusion criteria (from ~30% to >60% after attenuation corrections in selective reanalyses).
- Some cross‑sectional studies show weak relations between self‑reported practice hours and performance when instruction quality and other factors are controlled.
- Implication: an AI tutor should model multiple predictors (practice quality, cognitive aptitudes, motivation, instruction) rather than rely on raw practice hours alone.

7. Implications for an AI‑supported adaptive guitar and musicianship tutor (practical design recommendations)

Goal: help adult intermediate plateaued guitarists (2 hrs/day, ~1,400 hrs) progress to advanced musicianship across hearing, technique, rhythm, harmony, expression, repertoire and interactive skills.

7.1 Assessment battery (baseline and ongoing)

- Objective practice logging: automatic audio capture + time stamped activity labels (structured vs unstructured practice). Encourage user to enable audio capture for analysis; use privacy safeguards.
- Hearing/aural skills: interval/chord recognition tasks, melody recall, transposition tasks, and harmonic function identification measured with adaptive difficulty and delayed transfer tests.
- Technique: timed exercises (scale/sequence speed and accuracy), motion sensors (optional) for economy of motion, tone consistency measures (spectral analysis), and slow practice accuracy checks.
- Rhythm/timing: metronome alignment tests, polyrhythm identification, tapping synchronization, microtiming variability measures, and ensemble latency tolerance tests.
- Harmony: functional analysis tests, reharmonization tasks, voice‑leading exercises, and chord substitution drills with transfer to new progressions.
- Expression: recorded phrases evaluated by acoustic metrics (dynamics contour, spectral centroid variation) and AI scoring trained on expert exemplars; also subjective human scorer panels for calibration.
- Interaction: simulated duo/ensemble AI backtracks with controllable complexity; real partner logging for live sessions; call/response fluency tests.
- Injury/risk screening: self‑report Injury Risk Factors Questionnaire (Rousseau), practice load tracker, and posture tips. Remind breaks and warming routines (Chan & Ackermann recommendation: 5 min break per 25 min playing).

7.2 Pedagogy and curriculum sequencing

- Block 1 — Consolidate prerequisites: slow technique correction (motor imagery + slow practice), pulse stabilization, core repertoire expansion, targeted ear training tied to repertoire.
- Block 2 — Build templates & chunks: guided chunk extraction from repertoire, create contextual templates for common progressions, transcription tasks with adaptive scaffolding (start with phrase segmentation then full transcription).
- Block 3 — Rhythm and interaction: polyrhythm drills, ensemble simulations, progressive tempo variability, leader/follower exercises.
- Block 4 — Harmonic depth: functional reharmonization, voice‑leading labs, mode and substitution practice with immediate harmonic analysis feedback.
- Block 5 — Transfer and performance: mixed repertoire performance under pressure (recordings, live sessions), improvisation over unfamiliar changes, and compositional exercises to force recombination.

7.3 Practice session microstructure (2‑hour daily learner)

- Example daily split (two hours):
  - Warm‑up & prevention (10–15 min): mobility, targeted technique warmups, posture checks.
  - Deliberate technical work (30 min): slow, specific exercises with immediate feedback and micro‑goals.
  - Aural/harmony integration (20 min): ear drills directly tied to current repertoire/progressions.
  - Repertoire/phrase chunking (25 min): transcribe/learn 1–3 licks, practice chunk recombination.
  - Interaction / improvisation (20 min): ensemble simulation or live play, alternating leader/follower roles.
  - Cool down & reflection (10 min): log issues, pain, fatigue; set micro‑goals for next session.
- Insert 5‑minute micro‑breaks every 25 minutes as per Chan & Ackermann.

7.4 Feedback and personalization mechanisms

- Multi‑modal feedback: audio alignment (timing & pitch), spectral tone analysis, movement cues (if sensors available), and expert exemplar comparisons. Use automated scoring for routine metrics and human expert review for higher‑level musicality tasks.
- Adaptive difficulty: algorithmic challenge adjustment using performance metrics, introducing novel progressions and novel tempos if performance threshold is exceeded.
- Social features: scheduled duo matching for interaction practice, structured group sessions to provide ensemble pressure and novel contexts.
- Motivation: goal dashboards, periodization (mesocycles with focused theme), micro‑rewards, progress visualizations (learning curves with plateaus and accelerations flagged), and suggested public/recorded performance targets to trigger transfer.

7.5 Injury prevention features

- In‑app injury risk questionnaire (Rousseau style), micro‑break reminders (configurable), warm‑up/cool‑down routines, and empirical practice load monitoring. If symptoms reported, suggest immediate rest modifications and links to professional care.
- Encourage cross‑training and ergonomic suggestions; do not make unverified medical claims.

7.6 Data to collect for continual improvement and research

- Objective practice audio metadata (durations by task type), performance metrics (timing, pitch, error rates), subjective self‑reports (fatigue, pain, motivation), and validated assessment results.
- Use longitudinal mixed‑effects modelling to predict transfer and identify features most predictive of gains; avoid raw hour counts as sole predictor.

7.7 Evaluation plan for the tutor (research design)

- Randomized controlled trial (or waitlist control) with intermediate adult guitarists (N target 80–120) over 6–12 months measuring pre/post/retention/transfer across hearing, technique, rhythm, harmony and interaction. Include blinded raters for musicality and performance tasks, and instrumented metrics for objective changes.
- Collect practice logs (automated), baseline aptitude measures (working memory, prior training history), instruction quality proxies, and self‑efficacy/motivation questionnaires.

8. Practical, prioritized interventions for the plateaued learner (concrete)

Priority 1 — Stop mindless repetition: restructure sessions into deliberate micro‑tasks with immediate feedback and short blocks.
Priority 2 — Increase variability & transfer: practice unfamiliar progressions and modulate tempo/feel; schedule live duo sessions.
Priority 3 — Expand chunk/template libraries via focused transcription & contextual practice (learn and adapt short licks in multiple harmonic contexts).
Priority 4 — Strengthen rhythm & timing with adaptive metronome and polyrhythm drills; test with delayed transfer tasks.
Priority 5 — Injury prevention: adopt micro‑break schedule (5 min per 25 min), warm‑ups, and posture checks; self‑report any pain early and reduce load.

9. Limitations, knowledge gaps and recommended research

- Guitar‑specific empirical longitudinal studies are sparse: most quantitative results come from multi‑instrument musician samples or cross‑sectional designs. High‑quality randomized/longitudinal intervention trials specific to adult jazz/classical/pop guitar improvisation remain a gap.
- Injury prevalence evidence is heterogenous and often cross‑sectional; causal understanding and randomized interventions are limited.
- Expert vs novice comparisons often rely on small N qualitative work (Solstad’s 5 elite jazz guitarists) which is rich but not population‑representative.

Recommended studies to improve decision making for the tutor

- A longitudinal randomized trial of an AI‑supported deliberate practice tutor vs a control condition for adult intermediate guitarists (N>100), with objective practice logging and transfer measures, and 12 month follow‑up for retention.
- Ergonomic intervention trials for guitarists testing breaks, posture training and support devices with pre/post measures of PRMS/PRMD incidence.
- Multi‑site cross‑sectional studies combining objective practice logs (app data) with validated performance rubrics to refine variance‑explained estimates for practice quality vs quantity.

10. Final caveats and speculative points (flagged)

- Speculation: AI systems that provide high‑fidelity, immediate, multimodal feedback (audio, visual, movement) and scaffolded social interaction could significantly increase the efficacy of purposeful practice for adult amateurs; this is plausible but requires empirical validation.
- Caution: explained variance estimates for practice should not be interpreted as causal shares without longitudinal causal designs. High retrospective correlations can exist alongside large contributions of aptitude, instruction and chance.

Appendix: Practical checklists for the AI tutor team

A. Minimum viable assessments to implement immediately

- Baseline audio capture & segmentation (practice categories)
- Aural test battery (adaptive intervals/chords/tunes)
- Rhythm meter (metronome alignment + polyrhythm tests)
- Technique timed accuracy tasks
- Injury risk self‑report questionnaire

B. Quick algorithms and heuristics

- If user shows plateau (>4 weeks no improvement on objective metrics) — automatically switch to high‑variability deliberate practice block, schedule a live coach review, and introduce performance pressure (recording, streaming to friend group).
- If user reports pain — auto‑pause intensive tasks and deploy tailored warm‑down + reduced load plan; prompt medical referral if persistent >2 weeks.
- If user’s practice log is high (>90 minutes/day) but objective gains are low — flag for low‑quality practice: suggest session restructuring, targeted feedback tasks and transcription exercises.

C. Ethical and data considerations

- Ensure user consent for audio capture, anonymize data, provide opt‑out and local processing options; be cautious when suggesting medical advice — recommend professionals for diagnosis.

Closing summary

Adult intermediate guitarists who have plateaued can make measurable progress toward advanced musicianship when practice structure is improved: prioritize deliberate/purposeful tasks with immediate feedback, expand chunk/template libraries via transcription and contextual practice, develop rhythm and interaction skills through real or simulated ensemble work, and monitor and prevent injury through breaks and ergonomic screening. The empirical literature supports the importance of practice but shows significant heterogeneity in the size of its effect due to measurement and domain differences; therefore, an AI tutor must model practice quality, instruction, aptitude and motivation jointly. Finally, targeted field trials using objective logging and blinded performance measures are the highest priority to validate design choices for the tutor.
