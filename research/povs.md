# Spiky POVs — Guitar Plateau BrainLift

Andre chose these two POVs on 2026-10-08, replacing the earlier, tamer candidates in `pov-candidates.md`. Evidence IDs refer to `research/evidence.csv`, and every cited ID was re-read against its row and quote.

**Learner group (updated 2026-10-08).** Intermediate guitarists of any age, self-taught or lesson-taught, who are stuck on a multi-year plateau and determined to keep improving. Andre (an adult practising about 2 h/day) is the motivating real user, not the requirement. The POVs below did not change with this broadening. The pressure tests were run with the earlier "adult, 2 h/day for 2 years" context (see `pressure-tests.md`).

**Honesty note.** Only 8 of 97 evidence rows are *direct*, meaning guitar-specific evidence at intermediate level or above (any age), or guitar-specific feasibility. Four rows that were direct only because they studied adults are now *adjacent*. Most of what follows is *adjacent* (music, but another population or instrument) or *extrapolated* (lab motor tasks, non-music, novices, single sessions). Both POVs are bets that the evidence makes reasonable. Neither is a proven result for this learner group. `research/pressure-tests.md` records how each POV changed under pressure testing: v1 → v2 → v3 (final). The text below is v3.

## POV A — Hear it before you drill it

**One-line claim (final):** Hear it before you drill it. For a plateaued intermediate guitarist, no scale, lick or chord sound gets high-volume drilling until they can hear it and play (or hum) it back by ear. Scales and theory stay; the ear decides when they get drilled.

**The bet behind it:** the plateau is more often a hearing problem than a technique or theory problem. The tutor *tests* this bet for each learner instead of assuming it.

> **What v1 said and why it changed.** Andre's v1 said learners "usually have a hearing bottleneck" and that "no concept gets drilled until it is heard and sung/played back by ear."
> - No source in the register measures how common hearing bottlenecks are, so "usually" became a stated bet plus a diagnostic screen.
> - Pressure testing showed that a hard gate on *any* contact with the material would block the playing that builds hearing (E095, E096). The gate now covers *high-volume drilling and use in improvisation*. It does not cover first exposure, labelling or light playing.
> - Singing is accepted but not required. Playing back on the guitar counts.
>
> **v2 → v3.** The second pressure test found that guitar playback conflates *hearing* with *ear-to-fretboard mapping*, and mapping is partly built by repetition.
> - The screen and gate now include a non-instrument response: same/different discrimination, or hum/sing-back.
> - Mapping repetition (the material slowly in several positions and keys) is ungated, and so is slow, low-stakes improvisation.
> - The gate covers pitch and rhythm vocabulary only, not pure motor drills such as picking mechanics.
> - Passing requires two separate days, because a single 4-of-5 test is statistically noisy.
> - The gate applies to everyone (ear-strong learners pass quickly). The screen decides how much *time* goes to ear work.

**Consensus it opposes**
- **Theory-first / chord–scale-first.**
  - A jazz-education scholar describes the "main tendency" of formal jazz education as theory-based pedagogy, and quotes Monson that "Since the 1960s, jazz pedagogy has been dominated by the chord–scale approach" (E093, Bjerstedt 2016).
  - Professional jazz musicians interviewed in that work say chord/scale methods persist partly because they are "easy to verbalize and communicate" and "measurable to some degree" (E094).
  - Popular guitar advice repeats the theory-first framing. Examples are a music-school blog ("Theory knowledge accelerates the learning process") and a "theory tricks" YouTube lesson. Both are in the register flagged *exclude*, as examples of the advice, not as evidence (`wpma`, `yt_theorytricks`).
- **Scales-first / technique volume.** The popular reading of deliberate-practice research is "more focused hours". Practice does correlate with achievement (corrected r = .61, E042). But Ericsson's own definition requires individualized teacher guidance, not just volume (E046), and practice explains only 21–30% of variance in music (E043, E044).

**Counterpoint (the best case for the consensus)**
- Theory and fretboard labels can *train* perception: you hear the Dorian 6th once someone tells you to listen for it.
- Expert jazz guitarists describe their skill in terms of templates and domain knowledge (E071).
- Some plateaus are technical or rhythmic, and an ear gate does nothing for those.
- In one jazz-education study, the order of task types made no difference (E065, modal vs blues order, not ear vs theory).
- A randomized test of Gordon's audiation-based learning sequence found only an interaction effect (E074).

**Evidence for, with limits**

| Evidence | What it shows | Limits |
|---|---|---|
| E064 Brumbach 2022 | A practice-based approach beat a theory-based one on improvisation gains over 4 weeks | High school. Of N = 191, the improvisation comparison rests on **13 vs 21 soloists**, and the abstract does not describe the practice-based condition. We previously labelled it "aural", corrected 2026-10-08. In the jazz literature "practice-based" means recordings, transcription and imitation (Prouty, via Bjerstedt 2016). *Adjacent.* |
| E066 Laughlin 2001 | Aural exercises beat notated exercises for harmonic accuracy in beginning improvisers | n = 20, dissertation. The comparison is ear vs notation, not ear vs theory. *Adjacent.* |
| E067 Palmer 2016 | Aural imitation ability and technical facility are core correlates of jazz improvisation achievement | Correlational, n = 70, high school and college. *Adjacent.* |
| E073 (Kodály teachers), E072 (Karpinski) | Expert sequences put aural experience first (aural → kinesthetic → oral → visual); tonic hearing comes first in listening theory | Teacher perception and theory, no outcome data. |
| E075 Russell 2017 | Singing-based ear training helped tertiary jazz students recognise theory concepts in practice | Qualitative; the author evaluated their own method. |
| E095, E096 | Playing builds hearing: motor areas engage when listening to music one has learned to play, and play-plus-listen training beats listen-only | fMRI/MEG neural outcomes in non-musicians. *Extrapolated.* These **support ear–hand coupling, and argue against a hard ear-before-any-playing gate**, which is why v2 gates only volume. |
| E050 Hartz 2016 | Little research exists on how adult amateurs respond to ear-playing instruction | Gap statement. The study's own results were not captured. |

**Bottom line on evidence:**
- Direct evidence for POV A in intermediate guitarists (any age): **none found**.
- Adjacent evidence (small, short, school or college jazz studies) consistently favours ear- or practice-based approaches over theory- or notation-first ones.
- Nothing in the register measures how common hearing bottlenecks are, which is why the claim is framed as a bet with a screen.

**Practical implication: tutor**
- **Week 1 screen.**
  - (1a) same/different discrimination and hum-back of 2-bar melodies over a drone;
  - (1b) the same melodies played back on guitar;
  - (2) play back a 2-bar rhythm;
  - (3) a technique/speed check on a familiar scale.

  If 1b is weak but 1a is fine, the bottleneck is fretboard *mapping*, not hearing, so give mapping repetition. Weight time toward ear work only if 1a or 2 is the weakest. Otherwise say so honestly and route the learner to rhythm or technique work. The probes are on different scales, so the comparison needs norms from the pilot (`next-test.md`).
- **Gate rule.** A scale, arpeggio, chord quality, lick or progression enters tempo ladders and high-volume repetition only after the learner passes a by-ear check on two separate days, for example 4 of 5 short in-context examples (over a drone or chord) played or hummed back.
  - Pure motor drills (picking, legato, muting) are not gated.
  - Slow mapping work in several positions and keys is not gated.
  - Slow, low-stakes improvisation with the concept is not gated.
- **Before the gate opens:** label it, compare it with near neighbours (Dorian vs Aeolian), play it slowly while singing or humming, and do call-and-response. Light playing is allowed.
- **Gate checks are machine-scorable** because they use pitch and timing, which can be measured reliably (E086 pitch r = .93; E084 guitar transcription). Expressive quality stays off the gate.
- **Telemetry.** Log time-to-pass per concept type. Gates that stall (harmony, modes) get loosened.

**Practical implication: learner, tomorrow**
- Pick the scale or lick you have drilled most. Put on a drone or backing track, and have someone play (or play yourself, then stop) a 4-note fragment from it. Play it back without looking.
- If you can't do this reliably, stop drilling that pattern faster. Spend the time on call-and-response and singing-while-playing, slowly.
- Keep your theory: name what you just heard.

## POV B — Feedback after every take, never during

**One-line claim (final):** Feedback after every take, never during. Every full take gets feedback, right after the learner calls their own mistakes. "Withhold feedback so they don't depend on it" hasn't held up, and live on-screen feedback is out.

> **What v1 said and why it changed.**
> - v1 said reduced-feedback advice "doesn't hold" and that "complex skills benefit from 100% feedback". The evidence shows **no benefit of reducing feedback**. It does not show that 100% is superior (E026). E024 shows 100% beating *no* feedback, with 50% in between. So v2 claims "withholding doesn't help", not "more is better".
> - The strongest objection was dependency and outsourced self-evaluation. It is answered with a specific, evidenced moderator: when learners estimated their own error first, 100% feedback produced the *best* retention. Without that estimate it produced the *worst* (E097).
> - Feedback is limited to dimensions with demonstrated reliability, with one actionable priority per take. The other dimensions are offered for self-review, not scored.
>
> **v2 → v3.** The second pressure test's strongest objection was the *streetlight* problem: if only machine-measurable dimensions can fill the "one priority" slot, the tutor steers every take toward timing and pitch, even when the real limit is tension, time feel or tone. Revisions:
> - The priority slot can also hold a self-review target, or a technique item from periodic human or video review.
> - A "take" is one full run-through of a passage, exercise or piece, not every slow loop repetition.
> - "Couldn't assess this section" is shown, and kept distinct from "no error".
> - Metronome, drone and backing track stay. Only on-screen scoring while playing is banned.
> - The pilot checks that machine-metric gains track blind expert ratings (`next-test.md`).

**Consensus it opposes**
- **Give feedback sparingly / fade it.**
  - The guidance hypothesis: reduced or faded knowledge-of-results improved delayed retention in lab tasks (E023, Winstein & Schmidt 1990). Its origin is the review by Salmoni, Schmidt & Walter 1984, which is in the register but whose full text was not read.
  - Music pedagogy imported this as "feedback is given sparingly" (E030, Wulf & Mornell 2008).
- **Let learners choose when they get feedback** (E027, E030).

**Counterpoint (the best case for the consensus)**
- Frequent external feedback can replace the learner's own ear as the error detector, and they will practise and perform without the tutor.
- Moving feedback after the take doesn't avoid this risk. The original guidance-hypothesis studies used post-trial feedback.
- Plateaued intermediates may be on material that is no longer "complex" for them.
- Machine feedback is imperfect, and at 100% its false alarms add up.

**Evidence for, with limits**

| Evidence | What it shows | Limits |
|---|---|---|
| E026 McKay et al. 2022 | Meta-analysis (k = 75, N = 2,228): no significant effect of reducing feedback frequency at any time point | Shows no benefit of reducing feedback, **not** a benefit of 100%. The literature is severely underpowered. Lab motor tasks, *extrapolated*. Read from the preprint abstract. |
| E024 Wulf et al. 1998 | Complex ski-simulator skill: 100% concurrent feedback beat no feedback at retention, with 50% in between | n = 27 novices. **Feedback was concurrent**, so it doesn't support "never live" and says nothing about after-take feedback. No 100%-vs-50% test was reported. Row corrected 2026-10-08. |
| E025, E029 | Principles from simple tasks don't generalise to complex skills; feedback research is dominated by simple tasks | Narrative reviews. |
| E097 Guadagnoli & Kohl 2001 | 100% feedback plus self-estimation gave the best retention; 100% without estimation gave the worst | Simple force task, N = 64. *Extrapolated*, but it targets the exact dependency mechanism. |
| E028 McKay et al. 2022 | The self-controlled feedback benefit shrinks to g = .107 after bias correction | Supports default feedback over feedback "on request". Lab tasks. |
| E032, E033 Blanco 2021 | Real-time visual feedback for violin novices improved bowing movement but hurt sound *while on*; 55.2% "cannot play while watching" | Novices, a single session, kinematic feedback. **In the same-session no-feedback phase, the feedback group's sound was *better*.** So the case against live feedback rests on attention and in-practice sound cost, not on shown retention harm. |
| E068, E069 | Video self-review changed college guitarists' self-evaluation; weekly video recording helped adult beginner classical guitarists (p = .037) | E068 is the only *direct* row here (college-level guitarists, n = 16). E069 is *adjacent* (beginners; thesis; n not captured). Both are recording plus review, not AI feedback. |

**The reliability constraint (stated honestly)**
- Human raters are noisy:
  - two or three judges vary, and Bergee recommends five or more (E016);
  - experienced evaluators agree only moderately and show halo effects (E017, E018);
  - judges differ in severity and in what they attend to (E019–E021);
  - no published rubric differentiates criteria by learning stage (E022).
- Machines agree with experts on **pitch and rhythm** (erhu r = .93 and .83, E086; piano technique dimensions r = .74–.91, E085). They are **weaker at pinpointing which note went wrong** (problem-note F1 .705, E086).
- Guitar transcription is state of the art on GuitarSet (E081, E083), but a student tab pipeline reached only F1 .77, with pitch detection as the bottleneck (E084). These are clean benchmark recordings, not home recordings.
- **Implication:** "feedback on every take" is only as good as the dimension it reports. Per-take feedback must stick to timing, pitch and chord-change accuracy, plus whatever the learner flags themselves.

**Practical implication: tutor**
- **Every full take recorded.** A take is one run-through at the working tempo; slow micro-loops are not takes. Feedback comes after the take as a short card, in this order:
  1. The learner marks where they think they slipped and rates timing from 1 to 5 (self-estimate first, E097).
  2. The tutor reveals its analysis.
  3. The tutor shows **one** priority for the next take. The priority can be a machine-measured item, a self-review target ("listen for the attack on beat 3"), or a technique point from the weekly video or teacher check.
  4. Self-estimate accuracy is tracked as a skill in its own right.
- **No live visual display while playing.** A metronome or drone is allowed.
- Only validated dimensions are scored. Tone and phrasing appear as "listen back for…" prompts, not scores.
- Low-confidence detections are suppressed, but the section is marked "couldn't assess", never shown as clean.
- Self-vs-machine agreement is tracked over time. A weekly no-feedback take checks whether the learner's own error detection is improving.

**Practical implication: learner, tomorrow**
- Record every take on your phone.
- Before listening back, write down where you think you rushed or fumbled.
- Then listen (or run it through a transcription or timing tool) and check yourself. Pick one thing for the next take.
- Don't practise while watching a tuner or timing display.
